# Why a Good Workflow Fails When the Operating Routine Is Unclear

A well-designed workflow can fade within a few months of launch, even when the tool works and the team liked the idea. The usual cause is the absence of a clear, recurring moment when someone looks at the work and decides what happens next. This article defines the operating routine, names the signs that one is missing, and gives you a routine card you can fill in for any workflow.

## How good workflows fade

Imagine a regional title office that sets up an AI-assisted intake step. When a purchase contract arrives, the system extracts the parties, the property description, and the key dates, then prepares an opening checklist for a processor to review. The design is sound. It has a review point, an exception queue, and a named process owner.

For the first two weeks, everyone pays attention. In week three, a processor notices that the extraction keeps misreading a particular addendum. She mentions it in a hallway conversation. In week five, two processors start keeping their own spreadsheet alongside the checklist, because the spreadsheet is familiar. By week eight, the exception queue holds items nobody has opened, and the owner is surprised to hear that the team has mostly stopped using the tool.

Nobody decided to abandon the workflow. No single failure caused it. What was missing was a scheduled moment where the owner and the people doing the work looked at the output together, caught the addendum problem, and fixed it. Without that moment, small problems had nowhere to go, so people routed around them.

That moment is the operating routine.

## What an operating routine is

An operating routine is the recurring, scheduled review that keeps a workflow used and correct. It has a set time, set people, set inputs, a standing agenda, and a place where decisions get written down.

A status meeting asks people what they did. An operating routine inspects the workflow itself: what it produced, where the review points caught problems, what landed in the exception queue, and whether anything about the workflow should change.

The previous article in this series described the process owner, the one person accountable for how a workflow runs. The routine is where that accountability becomes a habit. The owner runs it. Without a routine, ownership lives only on paper.

Many workflows need three layers, each short:

| Layer | Who | Typical length | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily touch | The people doing the work | A few minutes | Clear the queue and flag anything unusual |
| Weekly review | Process owner and the people doing the work | 15 to 30 minutes | Exceptions, review-point corrections, the one measure, one change to try |
| Monthly or quarterly review | Process owner and executive sponsor | 30 to 60 minutes | Changes that need budget, scope decisions, whether to expand or retire |

The lengths are starting points. A low-volume workflow may need only the weekly layer. What matters is that each layer is scheduled and each has a job.

## Five signs the routine is unclear

These signs tend to appear before usage drops. Any two of them together usually mean the routine needs attention.

1. People ask where to check things. If team members are unsure where the queue lives or when it gets cleared, there is no shared habit around it.
2. The exception queue gets cleared when someone complains. Exceptions should be reviewed on a schedule, not in response to an escalation.
3. Changes happen in side conversations. A template gets edited, a step gets skipped, and nothing records it. Next month, nobody knows why the workflow behaves as it does.
4. The meeting reviews people instead of output. A small accounting firm might hold a weekly team meeting where each person reports progress on client files, while the AI-prepared reconciliation drafts never come up. The meeting exists. The routine does not.
5. Nobody can say when the workflow last changed. A workflow in active use gets adjusted. One with no recent changes is either perfect or unattended, and the second is more common.

## The routine card

The concrete tool here is a routine card: one page that says when the workflow gets reviewed, by whom, with what, and where decisions go. The example column is illustrative, for a hypothetical twelve-person HVAC service company that uses AI to draft follow-up quotes after service visits.

| Field | What to write | Illustrative example |
|---|---|---|
| Workflow | Name and trigger | Follow-up quotes drafted after each service visit |
| Process owner | One name, plus a backup | Service manager; office manager as backup |
| Cadence | Day, time, length | Tuesdays at 8:00, 20 minutes |
| Attendees | Who is expected | Service manager, office manager, one technician on rotation |
| Inputs | What is on the screen | Last week's drafted quotes, quotes heavily edited at review, quotes not yet sent, the exception queue |
| Standing agenda | The same four items each time | Exceptions; corrections made at review; the one measure; one change to try |
| Decision log | Where changes get recorded | A dated change log at the bottom of the workflow document |
| Skip rule | What a quiet week looks like | If the queue is clear and the measure is on track, end after five minutes, but still meet |
| Escalation | What goes up, and to whom | Tool or budget changes go to the owner's monthly review |

Three fields carry most of the weight. The inputs field keeps the review on the workflow's output rather than on anyone's performance. The decision log gives the workflow a memory. The skip rule keeps the routine alive in quiet weeks, which is when routines usually lapse.

## Designing a routine that lasts

A routine has to fit the calendar the team already has. A few practices help it survive.

- Attach it to an existing meeting where possible. Ten minutes at the end of a standing weekly meeting is easier to protect than a new meeting.
- Put the output on the screen. Look at the actual drafts, flags, and queue items. Summaries hide the problems the routine exists to catch.
- Bring one voice from the front line. Imagine a distributor with three warehouses reviewing an AI-generated report of mismatches between packing slips and purchase orders. If each warehouse lead brings one example from the week, the review stays grounded in what receiving actually saw.
- Decide one thing. A routine that ends with a recorded decision, even a small one, earns its place. A routine that ends with "let's keep an eye on it" usually fades.
- Watch for missed sessions. If the routine is skipped twice in a row, treat that as an exception in its own right and raise it with the sponsor.

## Where the routine meets property work

Commercial real estate shows why the routine matters, because much of the work carries a date and a counterparty. At a property management company, a weekly look at the work-order log and the next ninety days of lease dates catches problems while they are still small: a vendor who has not closed out a job, a notice window approaching, a certificate about to lapse. The same logic applies to any business where commitments have deadlines.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

On The Blvd runs on Command Platform, where lease terms and critical dates live once and feed every sheet that needs them. The board can show what is due. It cannot make anyone look at it. Some of the center's obligations sit years out: the reciprocal access and parking servitude with the corner bank parcel expires on 12/30/2034. A date that far away never surfaces in a weekly review, and it is exactly the kind of item that disappears without a routine built to reach it. So the routine has layers: a weekly look at work orders and near-term dates, and a periodic review that walks the long-dated obligations on purpose, with the owner and the operator reading the same board. The AI concierge can prepare a summary of what falls due in the next period and point to each governing document. A person reviews it and decides what happens next. For a center run under the Operator Landlord approach, the routine is where that active attention gets scheduled.

## The practical next step

1. Pick one workflow that has automation or AI in it. Write down when it was last reviewed as a workflow, rather than as a list of tasks.
2. Fill in the routine card for that workflow, including the skip rule and the decision log.
3. Attach the weekly review to a meeting you already hold, and run the first session this month.
4. Start the change log at that first session, even if the only entry is "no changes."
5. After four sessions, spend ten minutes reviewing the routine itself: what it caught, what it missed, and whether the cadence is right.
