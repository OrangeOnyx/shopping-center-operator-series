# From Pilot to Practice: What It Takes to Keep an AI Workflow Useful

A pilot gets attention. People watch it, talk about it, and fix it quickly when it stumbles. Practice is what remains once that attention moves on to the next priority. This closing article gathers the working vocabulary of the series into one pilot-to-practice checklist, so an owner can tell whether an AI-enabled workflow is ready to become part of how the business runs, and what to watch once it has.

## What changes when a pilot becomes practice

During a pilot, the workflow has sponsors. Someone checks the output every day because they want it to succeed. Problems get raised in the hallway and fixed the same afternoon. The measures, if there are any, are read eagerly.

None of that lasts on its own. Within a few months the sponsor has other work, the people who were excited have moved on to normal duties, and the workflow has to survive on whatever structure was built around it. If the structure is thin, the workflow drifts. Nobody decides to abandon it. It simply gets used a little less each week until a side spreadsheet quietly takes over.

The difference between a pilot and a practice is who notices when the workflow drifts, and what they do about it. That is an operating question, not a technology question. The model can be sound and the integration clean, and the workflow can still fade because no one was assigned to keep it useful.

The earlier articles in this series each addressed one part of that structure. The next section puts them in one place.

## The vocabulary, in one place

These are the terms the series has used. Each names a working part of an operating system.

- **Workflow map.** The written path of the work: trigger, person, information source, action, review point, decision, handoff. Before You Choose an AI Tool, Map the Work introduced it. Every other element hangs on it.
- **Smallest useful system.** One workflow, one owner, one measure, one review point. The Smallest Useful AI System Is Often the Best Place to Start argued for beginning here rather than with a platform.
- **Review point.** A deliberate place where a person checks, approves, or corrects AI-prepared work. AI may summarize, draft, compare, and flag. It does not decide or send on its own.
- **Exception queue.** Where work that does not follow the expected path lands, with a named owner and a response expectation. Design for the Work That Does Not Go According to Plan covered how to build one.
- **Process owner.** The one named person accountable for how the workflow runs from trigger to handoff, including its review points, its exceptions, and its changes.
- **Operating routine.** The recurring review that keeps the workflow used and improving: who meets, how often, what they look at, and what they decide.
- **Measures.** A few agreed operating counts, such as cycle time, rework, completion rate, response time, and review findings, taken against a baseline and kept in tools the team already uses.

## The pilot-to-practice checklist

Use the checklist below before declaring a pilot finished. Mark each line yes, partly, or no. A workflow with any "no" is still a pilot, whatever the calendar says. That is not a failure. It tells you what to build next.

| Element | Ready for practice when | Question to ask the team |
|---|---|---|
| Workflow map | The map reflects how the work runs now, including changes made during the pilot | Would a new hire understand the path from this map alone? |
| Scope | The system still covers one defined workflow, and new requests go to a list rather than into the build | What have we said no to, and where is that list kept? |
| Review point | A named person reviews AI-prepared work at a stated step, and what they correct gets recorded | When did the reviewer last change a draft, and what did they change? |
| Exception queue | Exceptions land in one place with an owner and an expected response time | How many items are in the queue now, and how old is the oldest? |
| Process owner | One person is named, has time assigned, and has authority to change the workflow | If the workflow stopped tomorrow, who would notice first? |
| Operating routine | A recurring review is on the calendar and has been held as scheduled | What did the last review decide? |
| Measures | A baseline exists, two or three measures are defined, and someone reads them in the routine | Did the numbers move since the baseline, and do we know why? |
| Records | The workflow's inputs and outputs live in one place people trust, and work is not being duplicated elsewhere | Is anyone keeping a side copy "just in case"? |

The last row is not a term from the vocabulary list, but it belongs on the checklist. A workflow can have every other element and still lose if the team does not trust its records. Once people start keeping private copies, the shared system stops being the source of truth.

## How workflows drift, and what to watch

Drift is gradual and usually polite. It rarely shows up as a complaint. It shows up as small changes in behavior that the measures and the routine are designed to catch. Three hypothetical examples show the common patterns.

Imagine a regional title office where AI prepares a first-pass summary of each title commitment for an examiner to review. In the pilot, examiners marked up every summary. A few months in, the review findings column reads zero week after week. That may mean the summaries improved. It may also mean the review became a glance. The process owner's job is to find out which, perhaps by spot-checking a sample.

Imagine a distributor with three warehouses using AI to flag orders likely to ship late. The exception queue grows steadily, and the oldest items are weeks old. The flags may be accurate, but no one has been assigned to act on them at the second and third sites. The fix is ownership, not a better model.

Imagine a small accounting practice where AI drafts client document requests. The operating routine moves from weekly to "when we have time," and then stops. Nothing breaks immediately. Six months later, nobody can say whether the drafts are still helping.

Each of these shows up on the checklist first. Reading the checklist once a quarter, after the pilot has ended, is a cheap way to catch drift before it becomes abandonment.

## Extend, hold, or retire

Once a workflow is in practice, each quarterly review should end with one of three decisions.

| Decision | When it fits | What happens next |
|---|---|---|
| Extend | Every checklist line is yes, the measures are stable or improving, and the owner has capacity | Map the next adjacent workflow and start a new smallest useful system. Do not bolt it onto this one. |
| Hold | The workflow works but one or two lines are partly | Fix those lines before adding scope. Most workflows spend most of their life here, and that is fine. |
| Retire | The measures show no gain over the baseline, or the work itself has changed | Document what was learned, return to the previous method or redesign from a fresh map. |

Retiring a workflow is a legitimate outcome. A business that can stop something that is not helping has an operating discipline worth more than any single tool.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

Command Platform, the software that runs On The Blvd Shopping Center in Lafayette, follows the same structure at the scale of one property. It was built around one real center, a roughly 63,000 square foot legacy multi-tenant asset on Arnould Blvd, and the build meant saying no to features that did not serve that center's actual work. That is scope discipline in practice. Each numbered sheet answers one operating question from one source of truth. The operator, the owner, vendors, and tenants each see a scoped view, which keeps ownership clear. The critical-dates board keeps dated obligations visible without anyone re-entering them. The AI concierge prepares drafts and points to the governing document, and it never sends. That is a review point built into the tool. Records persist and export, so the team has no reason to keep a side copy. None of this makes the system finished. It makes it something an owner can review, question, and improve on a routine, which is the whole point of this checklist.

## The practical next step

1. Pick one AI-enabled workflow that has been running for a while and score it against the checklist, line by line, with its process owner.
2. For every line marked partly or no, name one action and one person, and set a date to revisit it.
3. Put the checklist on the agenda of the operating routine once a quarter, even for workflows that score well.
4. At the next quarterly review, make an explicit extend, hold, or retire decision, and write down why.
5. If you choose to extend, start the next workflow from a fresh workflow map, and keep it to the smallest useful system.
