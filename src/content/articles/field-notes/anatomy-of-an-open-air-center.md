# The anatomy of an open-air shopping center

An open-air shopping center is a small commercial campus: one or two buildings, a shared parking field, a pylon, and a handful of tenants who live or die on the same curb cuts. It is not an enclosed mall. It is not a 400,000-square-foot power center with a Target and a sea of asphalt. It is the 40,000–90,000 SF neighborhood strip you already know — grocery-adjacent, restaurant-heavy, or a mixed service row on a state highway.

On The Boulevard in Lafayette is a clean specimen. Two buildings. 27 demised units. 62,883 square feet of GLA on 4.84 acres, zoned CH, addressed 101–149 Arnould Blvd. Long building on the west, short building on the north. Storefronts face a main parking field. Service happens on the back street. That is the whole diagram.

## The four pieces that actually matter

Land, building, parking, and access. Everything else — signage, landscaping, HVAC, CAM — is a consequence of those four. If you cannot draw those four from memory after a site walk, you do not know the asset yet.

Land is not “4.84 acres.” Land is parcels, easements, a remote overflow lot, a sold-out bank notch that is not yours, and a church waiver that still governs liquor even if the rent check stops. Building is not “62,883 SF.” Building is demising walls, HVAC assignments, and which units back a public right-of-way.

## Why neighborhood scale is a different sport

At this size, one exclusive-use clause can freeze a whole category. One parking variance can cap how much restaurant you can put on the field. One anchor HVAC covenant can dictate your vendor list. Institutional playbooks written for 250,000 SF centers will over-staff you and under-read the plat.

The job is not to make the center look like a mall. The job is to run a small, constrained, highly local operating system — and to know which constraints are legal, which are physical, and which are just habits.
