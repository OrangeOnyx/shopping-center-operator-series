# Shopping Center Formats: Where a 63,000 SF Neighborhood Center Fits

"Shopping center" covers everything from a four-tenant corner strip to an enclosed regional mall. Those are not the same business. The format you own determines who your tenants are, how lenders underwrite you, how much operating attention the asset needs, and what buyers will pay for it. This article walks the standard format ladder and locates a roughly 63,000 square foot legacy center on it.

## The format ladder

The industry, following conventions popularized by the International Council of Shopping Centers (ICSC), generally sorts open-air retail into a handful of formats. Boundaries vary by source and market, so treat the square footage ranges as guideposts rather than law.

- **Convenience / strip centers** — typically under 30,000 square feet. A row of small shops, often unanchored or anchored by a convenience use. Tenants are service and food businesses: nail salons, quick-service restaurants, dry cleaners. The customer trip is short and frequent.
- **Neighborhood centers** — roughly 30,000 to 125,000 square feet, traditionally anchored by a supermarket or a strong junior anchor such as a pharmacy. They serve daily needs for the surrounding neighborhoods, and the anchor's traffic feeds the inline tenants.
- **Community centers** — roughly 125,000 to 400,000 square feet, anchored by a discount department store, large grocer, or category retailer. Wider trade area, larger tenants, longer lease terms.
- **Power centers** — typically 250,000 square feet and up, dominated by several big-box "category killers" with minimal small-shop space. The anchors are the draw; inline space rides along.
- **Regional and super-regional centers** — 400,000 square feet to well over a million, anchored by full-line department stores, historically enclosed. A different asset class with different economics, and largely outside this series' scope.

Format is not just size. It is the function the center serves in its trade area: daily needs, weekly needs, or destination shopping. That function drives everything downstream.

## Anchor and inline: two kinds of space

Within any anchored center, space divides into two economic categories.

**Anchor space** is the large-format tenant — grocer, pharmacy, discount store — whose name belongs on the pylon sign and whose traffic justifies the center's existence. Anchors typically pay lower rent per square foot, sign long leases, negotiate heavily, and often control aspects of the center through their leases: exclusive uses, co-tenancy clauses, parking ratios, approval rights over changes. Losing an anchor is the defining risk event of an anchored center; a co-tenancy clause can even give inline tenants rent relief or termination rights when an anchor goes dark.

**Inline space** is the row of smaller suites between and around the anchors. Inline tenants pay higher rent per square foot, sign shorter leases, and depend on the anchor's traffic. Inline space is where most of the rent per square foot lives — and most of the leasing, turnover, and tenant-relations work.

A center's character comes from the interaction: the anchor brings the customer to the property; the inline mix determines whether that customer stays, spends, and returns.

## Why format drives leasing, financing, and operations

**Leasing.** A neighborhood center leases to daily-needs tenants and measures success in trips per week; a power center leases to destination retailers and measures in draw from miles away. The prospect list, the rent structure, the lease length, and the tenant-improvement expectations all differ by format. Pitching a regional destination retailer on your 63,000 square foot neighborhood center wastes everyone's time; so does underwriting your inline rents against power-center comps.

**Financing.** Lenders underwrite format. A grocery-anchored neighborhood center with essential daily-needs tenancy is often viewed as more durable through economic cycles than a center dependent on discretionary destination retail — though terms vary by market, lender, and the specific rent roll. Unanchored strips may face tighter leverage. Lenders typically look closely at anchor lease terms, remaining term, and sales health when the anchor dominates the income.

**Operations.** Operating intensity rises as tenancy gets smaller and more numerous. A single-tenant net-leased building needs almost no daily attention. A multi-tenant neighborhood center needs everything this series covers: common-area maintenance, CAM reconciliation, frequent renewals, vendor coordination, parking lot management, and constant leasing readiness. Format tells you how much of an operator you must be.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

On The Blvd Shopping Center sits at 101–149 Arnould Blvd in Lafayette, Louisiana — approximately 63,000 square feet of multi-tenant legacy retail owned by Belle Realty of Lafayette, LLC. On the format ladder, that square footage places it squarely in the neighborhood-center band: large enough to support a meaningful anchor presence and a real inline mix, small enough to remain a daily-needs, local-serving asset rather than a regional destination. The multi-tenant structure means the operating workload is real — many leases, many relationships, common areas that must earn their keep every day. The legacy character means the center has already proven its function to its trade area over time; the operator's job is to keep the format's promise — convenient, useful, well-kept daily retail — rather than to reposition it into something it is not. Knowing the format answers the strategy question before it is asked: this is a neighborhood center, and it wins by being the best-run neighborhood center on its corridor.

## Matching strategy to format

The practical discipline is alignment. Four questions keep a strategy honest:

1. **What function does the center serve?** Daily needs, weekly needs, or destination. Your tenant mix, hours, and marketing should match.
2. **Who is the traffic engine?** If there is an anchor, its health is your health. If there is not, the collective draw of the inline mix is the engine, and vacancy hurts faster.
3. **What does the format imply about rent durability?** Daily-needs and service tenancy tends to be stickier; discretionary destination retail is more cyclical. Underwrite accordingly.
4. **What operating intensity does the format demand?** Count the leases. Each one is a relationship, a renewal cycle, and a share of the CAM reconciliation.

## Formats drift — the legacy question

One caution deserves its own section: formats are not permanent. Corridors move, anchors come and go, and a center built as one format can find itself functionally serving another decades later.

A legacy neighborhood center may have lost its original anchor years ago and quietly become, in practice, a service-and-food strip with unusually deep suites. A former discount-anchored community center may now draw only from a few miles. This drift is not failure; it is information. The mistake is operating the format on the sign rather than the format in the trade area — leasing to daily-needs tenants while underwriting community-center rents, or holding out for an anchor that the corridor no longer supports.

The honest exercise is to classify the center as it functions today: who actually shops here, from how far, for what? Then let that present-tense answer — not the original site plan — drive the leasing list, the rent expectations, and the capital priorities. Legacy assets reward owners who update the classification without sentimentality.

## The practical next step

1. **Classify your center** against the format ladder — size, anchor presence, tenant function — and write the classification at the top of your ownership thesis (the subject of the next article in this series).
2. **Map your rent roll to anchor and inline.** What share of income and square footage is anchor-driven? What happens to the inlines if the anchor leaves?
3. **Read your anchor's lease** for exclusives, co-tenancy clauses, and approval rights. These provisions quietly constrain your leasing options.
4. **Benchmark against same-format comps only.** Rents, occupancy costs, and cap rates from a different format will mislead you.
5. **Let format set your operating calendar.** Multi-tenant neighborhood formats need the full operating rhythm described in this series — weekly walks, quarterly check-ins, preventive maintenance, and an eighteen-month renewal horizon.
