# Finding and Negotiating with Retail Tenants

Vacant suites do not get leased at the negotiating table. They get leased months earlier, in the canvassing, broker calls, and qualification work that determine who sits at that table. This article covers the full pipeline: where prospects come from, how to qualify them before you spend negotiating capital, how the LOI stage differs from the lease stage, how to frame economic tradeoffs, and the tracking discipline that keeps all of it from living in your head.

## Leasing is a pipeline, not an event

Operators who lease reactively — waiting for the sign to ring — negotiate from scarcity, with one prospect and no alternatives. Operators who treat leasing as a pipeline always have a second conversation running. The pipeline mindset changes behavior: you court prospects before suites are vacant, you keep conversations warm through long decision cycles, and you can walk from a bad deal because another deal exists.

A healthy pipeline for a small center might hold ten to twenty names per vacant or expiring suite, at stages from first contact to signed lease. Most will never convert. That is the point — volume at the top is what produces choice at the bottom.

## Building the prospecting engine

Prospects come from four channels. Work all of them.

- **Canvassing.** Walk the trade area and adjacent corridors. Note businesses with full parking lots, dated spaces, or concepts your mix lacks. A direct, respectful approach to a successful operator — "your customers already shop my center" — opens doors that listings never will.
- **Broker relationships.** Tenant-rep brokers control deal flow for regional and national concepts. Return their calls fast, keep your availability sheet current, and pay commissions promptly. Brokers route their best clients to landlords who make deals easy to close.
- **Signage.** A well-placed "space available" banner with a phone number still produces calls, especially from local operators who already drive past the center daily. Keep it professional and current; a faded sign markets the wrong message.
- **Local networks.** The chamber of commerce, economic development offices, lenders, accountants, and franchisee networks all know who is expanding before it is public. Show up consistently and tell people what you are looking for — by category, not just "tenants."

## Qualifying before negotiating

Negotiating with an unqualified prospect wastes months and burns your suite's market freshness. Qualify on three axes before any LOI.

**Business model.** Who is the customer, why this corridor, and does the concept fit the trade area's demographics and the center's mix? Ask for their reasoning; strong operators have done the homework.

**Capital.** Can they fund the buildout and survive the ramp? Ask about funding sources, other locations' performance, and what happens if the first year runs at 70% of plan. A tenant who runs out of money at month eight is a vacancy with legal fees.

**Experience.** First location, second location, or tenth? First-time operators are not automatic declines — local concepts often anchor neighborhood centers — but they warrant deeper diligence: references, a real business plan, and lease structure (shorter initial term, stronger guarantee) that shares the risk honestly.

Two cheap diligence steps punch above their cost. Visit the prospect's existing location unannounced at a busy hour: cleanliness, staffing, and customer flow tell you more than a pitch deck. And call references — prior landlords, their banker, a franchisor if relevant. Prior landlords are the most candid source in the industry; ask whether they would lease to this operator again and whether the bills were paid on time. A prospect who hesitates to provide references has answered the question.

Document everything in the pipeline record. The qualification notes you write today become the institutional memory that keeps you from re-courting a declined prospect two years from now.

## LOI stage versus lease stage

The letter of intent is where economics are decided; the lease is where they are documented. Keep the stages distinct.

The LOI is non-binding and short: rent, term, escalations, TI or landlord work, free rent, use and any exclusive, options, guarantees. Resolve the hard terms here — use scope, exclusivity boundaries, option rent mechanics — because they are far harder to reopen inside a forty-page lease. Do not let an LOI drift: set a response deadline and a target lease date, and keep marketing the suite until the lease is signed. Deals die quietly; suites should not.

At the lease stage, your counsel converts the LOI to legal form. Surprises at this stage — a tenant's attorney renegotiating agreed economics — are a signal about the tenant. Hold the LOI line or walk while the pipeline still has alternatives.

## How long leasing actually takes

Plan the calendar honestly. A local service tenant moving into a second-generation space might go from first conversation to opening in four to six months. A national concept with broker representation, committee approvals, and a full buildout can take twelve to eighteen. Timelines vary by market and concept, but the stages are consistent: prospecting and qualification, LOI negotiation, lease drafting and legal review, permits, buildout, and opening. Each stage has its own failure modes, and each month of delay is real rent lost.

Two implications follow. First, start early — the pipeline work for a lease expiring in eighteen months begins now, not at expiration. Second, sequence the work so stages overlap: the space plan and pricing should be ready before the LOI is signed, and your standard lease form should be current before you need it. The operators who lease fastest are rarely the most aggressive negotiators; they are the ones whose preparation removed the waiting.

## Negotiating the economics: tradeoffs, not concessions

Every economic term trades against another. Rent, TI, free rent, and term length are four dials on one machine, and a good negotiation moves them together rather than giving on one at a time. An illustrative framing for a 2,000 SF suite:

| If the tenant asks for… | The landlord trades for… |
|---|---|
| $15,000 more TI | Higher rent or a longer initial term |
| Six months free rent | Free period outside the term, or stepped rent later |
| A shorter term | Higher rent, fewer landlord concessions |
| A broad exclusive | A narrower scope, or higher rent for the risk |
| Renewal options | Option rent set at market with defined floors |

The discipline is knowing your walk-away economics before the first counter: the minimum rent the suite must carry, the maximum TI the returns allow, and the term your financing plans require. Inside those bounds, be flexible; at the bounds, be plain. Tenants respect a landlord who explains the trade — "I can fund the buildout if the term supports it" — more than one who simply says no. And document every concession against something received; a concession given without a trade teaches the other side to keep asking.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

At a 63,000 SF center like On The Blvd in Lafayette, the operator often knows the best prospects personally — they are running successful businesses ten minutes away. That is the Operator Landlord advantage: canvassing is not a campaign, it is Tuesday. The pipeline discipline still applies. A relationship is not a signed lease, and a friendly negotiation still needs the tradeoffs on paper.

## The practical next step

1. Open a simple pipeline tracker — spreadsheet or CRM — with columns for prospect, suite, stage, next action, and date. Every prospect goes in, no exceptions.
2. Review the pipeline weekly: what moved, what stalled, who needs a call.
3. Canvass one adjacent corridor this month and add three names to the list.
4. Call two tenant-rep brokers and tell them exactly what categories you want.
5. Write down your walk-away economics for each vacant or expiring suite before the next negotiation starts.
