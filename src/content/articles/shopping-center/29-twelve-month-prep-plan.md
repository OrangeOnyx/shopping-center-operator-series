# The 12-Month Prep Plan: Maximizing Value Before a Sale

A shopping center sells for a multiple of its income and a discount for its uncertainty. The twelve months before a sale are your chance to raise the first and shrink the second. This is not about cosmetic flips — buyers underwrite through paint. It is about NOI quality: income that is real, documented, collectible, and likely to continue. Here is a month-by-month plan a small owner-operator can actually execute.

## Why twelve months

Buyers price what they can verify, and verification runs on documents: leases, ledgers, reconciliations, estoppels, maintenance history. Most of those documents take months to produce properly because they take months to become true. A clean CAM reconciliation for last year cannot be created the week before listing — it either happened or it did not. A renewed anchor-adjacent tenant needs a signed amendment, not a verbal assurance.

Twelve months also matches how buyers read trailing financials. A buyer's underwriter will study the trailing twelve months of income and expenses. The year before you go to market is the year that will be studied. Run it like it matters, because it does.

The plan below assumes an owner-operator with a day job. Each phase is a few focused workstreams, not a second career.

## Months 1–3: clean the books

The first quarter is about making the numbers trustworthy — to a buyer, but first to yourself.

- **Reconcile the general ledger to the rent roll.** Every tenant, every charge, every month. Discrepancies between what the lease says, what was billed, and what was collected are normal in owner-operated books; they are also what buyers' analysts find first and price worst.
- **Close out CAM reconciliation properly.** If CAM reconciliations have been loose — estimates never trued up, categories blurred — fix the current year and restate the prior year cleanly. CAM reconciliation (the annual true-up of estimated common-area maintenance billings against actual costs) is where retail buyers most often find leakage and disputes.
- **Organize the lease files.** For every tenant: the lease, all amendments, guaranties, insurance certificates, commencement letters, and correspondence that changes anything. A missing amendment discovered in due diligence reads as a missing liability.
- **Separate property from personal.** Owner expenses run through the property — vehicles, travel, family payroll — are common in small operations. Clean them out of the property P&L now so the income you present is defensible line by line.

Deliverable for the phase: a rent roll you would sign your name to, and twelve months of P&L that tie to bank deposits.

## Months 4–6: fix what buyers discount

Buyers do not deduct the cost of deferred maintenance from their offer. They deduct the cost plus a risk premium plus a negotiating margin. Triage beats renovation: spend where the discount exceeds the cost.

- **Life-safety and water first.** Roof leaks, trip hazards, lighting failures, drainage problems. These kill deals in diligence and poison inspections. Fix them regardless of sale timing — they are operating obligations.
- **Lighting.** A full lot relight is one of the highest-leverage pre-sale spends at a legacy center: visible in every site visit, visible in every photo, and priced modestly against its effect.
- **Signage.** Replace dead panels and departed-tenant names. A sign system that is current reads as an owner who is current.
- **The first-impression loop.** Entrance, landscaping at the road, striping at the main drive, facade touch-up at eye level. This is the path every buyer walks in the first ten minutes, and first impressions set the tone for how the rest of diligence is read.

What to skip: full interior renovations of occupied space, speculative build-outs, and cosmetic projects whose cost you will not recover in price. NOI quality over cosmetic flips is the rule. A buyer pays for durable income and reduced risk, not for your taste.

The triage math, stated illustratively: imagine a roof section that needs $40,000 of work. A buyer's underwriter who discovers it in diligence rarely deducts $40,000. They deduct the repair cost plus contingency for what else the roof might be hiding, plus a margin for the hassle of doing the work themselves, plus whatever the discovery does to their confidence in the rest of your numbers. A $40,000 repair left undone can easily become an $80,000 price conversation. The same logic runs in reverse for cosmetics: $40,000 of interior finish-out in an occupied suite adds nothing the buyer can underwrite, because the income was already there. Spend where the discount exceeds the cost; skip where the cost exceeds the story it tells.

## Months 7–9: stabilize income

With books clean and the physical story repaired, the third quarter is about the income itself.

- **Renew early.** Any tenant you want to keep with an expiration inside the next eighteen months gets a renewal conversation now. A signed renewal converts a buyer's risk line into income; a pending conversation does not. Accept slightly softer terms in exchange for term and certainty where the trade makes sense.
- **Fill strategic vacancy.** Not every vacancy is equal. A small shop space that has sat dark hurts more than its lost rent because it photographs badly and signals leasing weakness. Filling it — even at a modest rent with a modest term — can return more in sale price than it does in income. Deep discounts for a marquee name, by contrast, rarely pencil.
- **Collect the arrears.** Old receivables either get collected, settled and documented, or written off cleanly. A rent roll showing chronic delinquency invites buyers to underwrite your other tenants more harshly.
- **Document the wins.** Renewals signed, occupancy improved, a problem tenant resolved. The buyer will hear the story either way; make sure the record supports the good version.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

At a roughly 63,000 SF legacy multi-tenant center like On The Blvd at 101–149 Arnould Blvd in Lafayette, Louisiana — owned by Belle Realty of Lafayette, LLC and run under the Operator Landlord approach — this phase is where the operating philosophy pays out directly. A center this size lives or dies on a handful of tenancies, so each renewal is material, and a landlord who has spent years in active partnership with tenants is negotiating renewals with people who already trust the relationship. The twelve-month plan is not a departure from normal operations; it is normal operations with the documentation turned up.

## Months 10–12: package the story

The final quarter converts a well-run year into a well-presented asset.

- **The rent roll, final form.** Tenant, suite, square footage, rent, escalations, reimbursements, expiration, options, deposits — every field complete and tied to the lease files.
- **The financial package.** Trailing twelve months and two prior years of income and expense, in a format a buyer's analyst can work from, with one-time items identified and explained.
- **The capital history.** A schedule of major repairs and replacements — roofs, lot work, HVAC, signage — with dates and costs. Capital history converts "legacy" from a liability word into a stewardship story.
- **The property file.** Survey, title documents, environmental reports, warranties, service contracts, zoning and permit records. Assemble it once, completely, and the offering memorandum and diligence room build themselves.
- **The narrative, in writing.** One or two pages: what the center is, who shops there, what has been done, what the opportunity is. The broker will write the OM, but the owner supplies the truth in it.

## The practical next step

1. Pull the current rent roll and reconcile it against leases and bank deposits — note every gap.
2. Commission the last CAM reconciliation properly and close out any open true-ups.
3. Walk the property with a buyer's eyes and list every life-safety, lighting, and signage defect; price the fixes.
4. List every lease expiring in the next eighteen months and rank them: renew, re-lease, or let go.
5. Open a digital folder for the property file — leases, financials, capital history, title, survey — and start filling it this week.
