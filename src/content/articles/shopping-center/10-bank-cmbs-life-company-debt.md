# Bank, CMBS, or Life Company: Choosing the Right Retail Loan

The question is not which lender is best. It is which lender fits this asset, this business plan, and this balance sheet. A local bank, a CMBS conduit, and a life insurance company are underwriting three different products, and the differences show up in leverage, recourse, flexibility, and the servicing experience you will live with for years. This article compares the main retail capital sources and offers decision guidance for a typical neighborhood-center deal. Note the framing throughout: pricing and terms move with markets, nothing here is a rate quote, and terms vary by market, lender, and quarter.

## The lender landscape

**Local and community banks.** Relationship lenders that hold loans on their own balance sheets. They know their trade area, move quickly, and can flex on structure. The tradeoffs: recourse is standard, terms are typically shorter — five years is common — and maximum leverage is often more conservative. For a neighborhood center with a local story and a borrower the bank knows, this is frequently the natural first call.

**Credit unions.** Similar balance-sheet lending with membership-based incentives that can produce competitive pricing on smaller deals. Capacity and product range vary widely; some are active commercial real estate lenders, others are not.

**CMBS conduit loans.** Loans originated to be pooled and securitized. CMBS typically offers higher leverage and non-recourse structure at fixed rates for five to ten years. The tradeoffs are rigidity and servicing: once securitized, modifications go through a servicer bound by pooling rules, and requests that a bank would handle in a phone call — an easement consent, a lease approval — can take weeks. Defeasance prepayment makes early exits expensive. CMBS works best for stabilized assets where the plan is to hold and collect, not to reposition.

**Life insurance company loans.** Balance-sheet lenders with long time horizons. Life companies generally favor quality: well-anchored, well-located assets at moderate leverage, priced attractively for the strength of the collateral, with longer terms available. They are selective; a center with significant near-term rollover or a heavy value-add plan is usually not their target.

**Agency debt.** Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac programs are built for multifamily. Retail is not a typical agency asset class — worth knowing mainly so no one wastes time chasing it.

**Private lenders and debt funds.** Non-bank lenders pricing speed, flexibility, and risk tolerance into the rate. Appropriate for transitional assets — vacancy to fill, an anchor to replace, heavy renovation — where the business plan, not current income, is the story. The cost of capital is higher; the value is certainty and speed of execution on deals banks will not touch yet.

## Side-by-side comparison

The table below is **general and illustrative** — typical tendencies, not commitments. Every category contains wide variation.

| Dimension | Community bank | Credit union | CMBS | Life company | Debt fund |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Typical leverage | Moderate | Moderate | Higher | Lower–moderate | Higher |
| Pricing tendency | Competitive, relationship-based | Competitive on smaller deals | Competitive at higher leverage | Attractive for quality | Premium for speed/risk |
| Recourse | Usually full or partial | Usually full | Non-recourse (carve-outs) | Usually non-recourse | Varies |
| Flexibility | High | Moderate | Low | Low–moderate | High |
| Servicing experience | Direct, personal | Direct | Servicer-mediated | Direct, conservative | Direct, engaged |
| Speed | Fast for known borrowers | Moderate | Moderate | Deliberate | Fast |
| Best fit | Local stabilized or light value-add | Smaller local deals | Stabilized, hold-oriented | High-quality anchored assets | Transitional, heavy value-add |

## Fit by deal size and asset quality

Deal size sorts lenders before quality does. A loan in the low single-digit millions — the range of many neighborhood centers — is home turf for community banks, credit unions, and some debt funds, but below the efficient minimum for many conduits and life companies. As loan size climbs into the mid-single digits and beyond, CMBS and life companies become realistic options, with the choice driven by asset quality: stable, anchored, longer-WALT centers attract life-company attention; stabilized centers where leverage matters attract conduit quotes.

Asset quality then sorts within the size band. Stabilized with a plan to hold: bank or CMBS, priced against each other. Transitional with real work to do: bank if the relationship is strong and the recourse is tolerable, debt fund if not. Institutional quality with conservative leverage: life company.

## An illustrative decision walk

Consider a hypothetical ~63,000 SF neighborhood center in a market like Lafayette, Louisiana — legacy construction, multi-tenant, anchored by daily-needs retail, with some near-term rollover and a parking lot that will need capital within a few years. Loan need: modest single-digit millions.

A deal of that profile typically points first to **local and regional banks**. The size fits their appetite, the market is one they know, and the near-term rollover is a story a local lender can underwrite with a site visit and a conversation — especially for a borrower with recourse capacity and an operating track record. Pricing on bank debt for this profile is often competitive at moderate leverage; terms typically run shorter, so the borrower accepts refinance risk in exchange for flexibility and relationship.

**CMBS** is usually a weaker fit here: loan size may sit below conduit sweet spots, and the rollover plus planned capital work argues against locking into rigid servicing and defeasance. **Life companies** would likely want a stronger WALT and anchor profile than a legacy center with rollover presents. A **debt fund** would execute quickly but at a cost of capital that only makes sense if the bank route fails or timing demands certainty.

The honest guidance: run the bank process first, in parallel with a second option, and let real term sheets — not assumptions — make the decision. In many Gulf South markets, two or three bank quotes on a deal like this will tell you more than any comparison table.

## Reading the term sheet beyond the rate

When quotes arrive, the rate is the least interesting line on the page. Five other provisions decide what the loan is actually like to live with:

- **Proceeds and holdbacks.** Two quotes at identical rates can differ by hundreds of thousands in delivered proceeds once reserves, escrows, and closing holdbacks are netted out. Compare net funded dollars, not face amount.
- **Recourse language.** "Non-recourse" means nothing until you read the carve-outs. Count how many are drafted as the borrower's strict liability versus requiring fault, and note whether guaranties burn off at defined performance levels.
- **Covenant and trigger levels.** A debt service coverage trigger set at a level your pro forma barely clears is a lockbox waiting to spring. Model a bad year against every covenant before signing.
- **Approval rights.** Some loans require lender consent for leases above a size or rate threshold. On a center you plan to actively re-tenant, lease-approval drag is an operating cost — price it.
- **Servicing and assumption.** Who collects, who answers the phone, and can a future buyer assume the loan? Assumability can be a real selling feature at exit when rates have moved.

A disciplined comparison puts all five alongside rate and fees in a single spreadsheet. The cheapest quote frequently stops being cheapest by the third row.

## The practical next step

1. Map your deal's profile — size, WALT, rollover, capital needs — against the lender categories above before requesting quotes.
2. Approach at least two capital sources in parallel; competing term sheets are the only real pricing data.
3. Compare quotes on total economics: rate, fees, reserves, recourse, prepayment, and servicing obligations — not rate alone.
4. Read the servicing provisions of any securitized loan as carefully as the pricing; you will live with them longer.
5. Build one genuine banking relationship in your market this quarter, before you need the loan.
