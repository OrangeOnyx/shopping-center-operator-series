# Parking Is the Lease: How a 324/344 Variance Governs Every Deal

Every retail operator learns to read a rent roll. Fewer learn to read a parking approval, and at a legacy center that is the document that decides which leases you may sign. Zoning codes require a minimum number of parking spaces for the floor area a center contains, and the requirement changes with the use: a restaurant or a fitness studio typically needs more spaces per square foot than a boutique. When a center was built under an older code, or was approved with fewer spaces than the code demanded, the shortfall is usually resolved by a recorded variance with conditions attached. Those conditions do not expire. They sit in the public record and govern every lease, every change of use, and every proposal that would remove a stall. This article explains how to find the number, how to reconcile it against the lot you actually own, and how to run leasing decisions through it before a prospect is ever quoted a rate.

## The number that governs the center

A parking variance is an approval that lets a property operate with fewer spaces than the code requires, on stated conditions. It is recorded by entry number with the local planning authority. The three facts it contains are the ones an operator must memorize: the spaces **provided**, the spaces **required**, and the **condition** that ties floor area to parking.

At On The Blvd the variance is Entry 99-11797, approved in 1999. It states 324 spaces provided against 344 required, and it conditions the center's floor space as "limited to available parking spaces." Read that phrase slowly. It does not say the center may operate at a 20-space deficit forever. It says the usable floor area is capped by the parking that exists. Add a use that consumes more parking, or remove stalls for a patio, and the approval is the document a neighbor, a lender, or a code officer will reach for.

Three consequences follow for leasing:

- **Use matters as much as square footage.** A suite that parks at one ratio as retail may not park at all as a restaurant under the same approval. The prospect's use has to be tested before terms are discussed.
- **Stalls are inventory.** Every proposal that removes a stall — outdoor seating, a drive-through queue, a dumpster relocation, a monument sign — spends inventory the variance already counts as committed.
- **The count is legal, not descriptive.** The variance number is what you cite in a lease, a license, or a dispute. Whether it matches the striped lot is a separate question, and at a legacy center it usually does not.

## Reconcile the approval against the plat

The second number an operator needs is the count the recorded plat actually labels. Plats are revised over decades; the striping shown on the latest revision is what a surveyor certified, zone by zone. Tally those labels and compare them to the variance.

At On The Blvd the plat's own labels total 314 spaces across nine zones — the main field, the storefront row, the frontage rows on two streets, a corner pocket lot, a rear parallel row, a remote lot across the side street, and 13 spaces on the neighboring bank parcel that a reciprocal servitude lets the center count. The variance says 324. The ten-space gap sat unexplained for years.

The candidate answer came from the architect's CAD drawing: it stripes a ten-stall head-in row on the highway frontage that the plat never labels with a space count. 314 labeled plus 10 unlabeled equals 324 — the variance figure exactly. Aerial imagery shows cars parked there. Until the stalls are counted on the ground, the operating rule is conservative: cite 324 in legal and leasing documents, because it is the recorded figure, and plan operations on 314, because that is what the plat certifies. Any proposal that removes stalls is tested against the larger deficit. Required minus striped is 344 less 314, a 30-space effective shortfall, not the 20 the variance implies.

### A reconciliation checklist

- Pull the variance file by entry number and read the site plan exhibit attached to the application. Its stall layout is the authoritative "provided" count.
- Tally the latest recorded plat's striping labels zone by zone. Note which zones the plat labels and which it merely draws.
- Compare the architect's construction drawings, if you have them, against the plat. Unlabeled rows and re-stripes hide there.
- Identify every space you count that sits on land you do not own — easement spaces, remote lots, shared drives — and calendar the instrument that grants them.
- Record the reconciled figure, the delta, and the rule you will operate under until the delta closes.

## Run every deal through the parking test

Once the numbers are known, the leasing test is mechanical. Before a letter of intent goes out, three questions get written answers.

**Does the prospect's use change the ratio?** Many codes park food service, fitness, medical, and assembly uses more intensively than general retail. If the proposed use parks harder than the current one, the center's total requirement rises, and a center already operating under a variance has no cushion to give. The answer may be no, or it may be a conditional yes with hours, seating caps, or a written parking plan.

**Does the deal remove or reserve stalls?** Reserved parking, outdoor seating, and delivery zones all spend stalls. Draw them on the plan and subtract them from the operating count before agreeing to anything.

**Does the deal rely on borrowed parking?** Spaces on a neighbor's parcel, or on a remote lot, exist only as long as the instrument that grants them. If a reciprocal easement expires in a stated year, every lease that depends on those spaces should end, or be renegotiable, before that date.

The output of the test is a one-page parking memo attached to the deal file: current requirement, current supply under both counts, the effect of the proposed use, and the stalls the deal consumes. Write it before the terms conversation, not after. A prospect who hears "we need to check parking" after agreeing on rent hears a landlord who does not know their own property.

## Parking as a capital and operating asset

Because stalls are inventory, the lot deserves the same planning as the roof. Restriping to current accessibility standards typically consumes spaces, since van-accessible pairs take more room than the stalls they replace. Adding landscape islands to meet a green-area requirement consumes spaces. A re-stripe done without counting can quietly push a center out of compliance with its own approval. The operator's rule is simple: no striping plan is approved without a before-and-after count, and the after count is compared to the variance before the contractor starts.

Operationally, the count also sets capacity for everything that is not tenant parking: church overflow, contractor staging during a roof replacement, food-truck events, a holiday tent sale. The conservative figure governs, and the borrowed spaces are excluded from any plan that runs past the instrument's expiry.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

On The Blvd Shopping Center, roughly 63,000 SF at 101–149 Arnould Blvd in Lafayette, operates under exactly this constraint: a 1999 variance of 324 provided against 344 required, a plat that certifies 314, a ten-space candidate reconciliation from the construction drawings, and 13 spaces that live on a neighbor's parcel under a servitude with a stated end date. Under the Operator Landlord approach the numbers live in the operating system, not in a filing cabinet — the site plan draws every zone with its count, the dashboard shows the variance from the instrument file rather than from memory, and the critical-dates board carries the servitude's expiry as a deadline. When a prospect asks about a patio, the answer starts with a stall count, and the stall count already exists.

## The practical next step

1. Find your parking approval by entry number and write the three facts — provided, required, condition — on the first page of your leasing binder.
2. Tally the recorded plat's striping labels by zone and record the delta against the approval, with your interim operating rule.
3. Write the one-page parking test and require it on every deal file before terms are proposed.
4. Calendar the expiry of every instrument that grants borrowed spaces, and tag the leases that depend on them.
5. Adopt the before-and-after count rule for any striping, island, or signage work on the lot.
