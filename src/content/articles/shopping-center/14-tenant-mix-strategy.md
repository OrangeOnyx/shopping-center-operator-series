# Tenant Mix Strategy: Building a Center That Feeds Itself

A fully leased center can still underperform if the tenants do not feed one another. Tenant mix is the deliberate design of which uses sit next to which, so that each trip generates a second trip and each tenant's customers lift the neighbors' sales. This article lays out the logic — anchors, the service-retail-food balance, conflict management, and succession planning — and gives you a simple scoring framework for evaluating the next prospect.

## Mix is a traffic strategy, not a leasing outcome

Two centers with identical occupancy can produce very different sales per square foot. The difference is trip logic. A center leased to whoever called first ends up with uses that compete for the same visit window, duplicate each other, or draw customers who have no reason to walk past another storefront. A designed mix creates chains: the grocery trip adds a pharmacy stop, the pharmacy stop adds a coffee, the coffee adds a haircut next week.

The practical consequence: sometimes the right answer to a willing tenant is no. A lease that fills a suite but breaks the trip chain — or blocks a better use through an exclusive — can cost more in center-wide sales than it adds in rent. This is also why tenant sales health matters to the landlord, not just to the tenant: a neighbor's strong sales are part of your next renewal conversation. Mix discipline is one of the few levers an operator controls that compounds over time.

## Anchors and junior anchors: the gravity model

Anchors generate gravity. In a neighborhood center, the anchor is often a grocer or a strong daily-needs draw; junior anchors — a pharmacy, fitness concept, discount retailer, or medical clinic — add a second and third reason to visit. The logic is frequency: anchor tenants pull weekly or near-weekly trips, and small shops live in the overflow.

Placement matters as much as category. Suites with the best visibility from the anchor's entrance and parking field command the mix premium; deep or hidden suites need destination uses that pull their own traffic. When an anchor weakens, everything downstream reprices — sales, renewal odds, and the rents small shops can carry. That is why anchor health is a center-level concern even though the anchor's lease may be the cheapest rent per foot in the property.

## The service-retail-food balance

Neighborhood centers run on daily-needs tenancy: uses people need every week regardless of the economy. Think groceries, pharmacy, quick-service food, personal services like hair and nails, medical and dental, fitness, pet care, banking. These uses are durable because they are hard to move online and easy to combine into one trip.

Balance has three dimensions:

- **Visit windows.** Coffee and breakfast uses draw mornings; lunch concepts midday; services and fitness evenings. A mix spread across dayparts keeps the lot active and shares parking load.
- **Complement versus duplicate.** Two sandwich shops split a finite lunch crowd; a sandwich shop beside a salad concept can grow it. Before signing a second use in a category, ask whether the trade area supports two.
- **Service density.** Service tenants — salons, clinics, agencies — pay reliable rent on modest sales, and their appointments create predictable repeat visits that retail feeds on.

There is no universal ratio; trade areas differ. The test is simpler: for each suite, can you name the tenant whose customers will walk past its door, and the tenant whose customers it will send onward? If you cannot answer both halves, the mix has a hole — and the hole, not the vacancy, is the problem to solve. Food deserves one more note: restaurants lease slowly and build out expensively, but a center with no food use surrenders the longest dwell-time trips in retail. Even one well-chosen quick-service tenant changes how long customers stay on the property.

## Managing use and exclusivity conflicts

Every exclusive you grant narrows your future leasing board. Manage them as a portfolio, not one lease at a time.

Keep a single exclusivity map: each protected use, its scope, its carve-outs, and which suites it blocks. Before quoting any prospect, check the map — conflicts discovered after an LOI cost credibility and sometimes money. When negotiating new exclusives, define the protected use narrowly ("sale of submarine sandwiches" rather than "fast food"), add carve-outs for existing tenants and their successors, and consider excluding incidental sales — a grocer's deli counter should not violate a sandwich exclusive if the clause is drafted with proportion in mind.

Watch the quiet conflicts too: co-tenancy clauses tied to anchor operations, prohibited uses in anchor leases, and parking ratios that a high-intensity restaurant can strain. Mix design is constrained design, and the constraints live in your existing leases.

## Scoring a prospect's fit

When a prospect appears, score it before negotiating. A simple six-factor framework, 0–2 points each:

| Factor | Question | 0 / 1 / 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Traffic contribution | Does it bring customers the center lacks? | Duplicates / neutral / new draw |
| Cross-shopping | Will its customers visit neighbors? | No / some / strong chain |
| Daypart fit | Does it fill an empty visit window? | Conflicts / overlaps / fills gap |
| Conflict check | Exclusives, co-tenancy, parking? | Violates / tight / clean |
| Operator strength | Experience and capitalization? | Thin / adequate / proven |
| Economics | Rent, term, and TI versus suite plan? | Below / at / above plan |

Ten or better: pursue. Seven to nine: negotiate for terms that fix the gap. Below seven: hold the suite for a better fit, unless vacancy pressure argues otherwise — an honest tradeoff worth stating out loud.

## Planning for succession

Every suite has a next tenant, whether or not you have chosen them. For each space, keep a short backfill list: two or three categories, and named prospects where possible, that fit the mix if the current tenant leaves. Update it when leases approach expiration or a tenant's performance slips — declining sales, late payments, and neglected storefront upkeep are early signals an operator learns to read.

Succession also means lease design. When a departing tenant's lease carried an exclusive or an unusual use clause, decide before the space goes dark whether that clause survives into the next deal or ends with the old one. And when a key tenant gives notice, communicate with the neighbors honestly: small shops watch an emptying suite with anxiety, and a landlord with a named plan keeps renewal conversations calm. Finally, budget the turnover cost — downtime, backout work, new tenant improvements, and commissions — so the succession plan is funded, not just imagined.

When a departure happens, succession planning converts a vacancy from a scramble into an execution step — and protects the trip chains the rest of the tenancy depends on.

## The practical next step

1. Write down the trip chain for your center as it exists today — where does the typical visit start, and what does it add?
2. Build the exclusivity and co-tenancy map before your next leasing conversation.
3. Score your current tenancy with the six-factor framework; the low scores are your watch list.
4. Draft a backfill list for every suite with a lease expiring within 24 months.
5. Name the one category your mix is missing, and start looking for it deliberately.
