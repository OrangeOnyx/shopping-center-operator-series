# Renewals and Retention: Keeping Good Tenants Longer

Every shopping center owner knows the math instinctively: keeping a good tenant is cheaper than finding a new one. Yet renewals are where discipline most often breaks down. The lease expires, everyone scrambles, and the negotiation happens under time pressure that serves nobody. Retention is not a negotiation skill. It is a calendar, a habit of early conversation, and a clear-eyed view of which tenants deserve the effort. This article lays out that system.

## Retention is the cheapest leasing you will ever do

Price out a vacancy honestly and the case for retention makes itself. When a tenant leaves, you lose rent for the downtime — months, often quarters, in many neighborhood-center markets. You pay leasing commissions on the replacement. You may fund tenant improvements and a period of free rent. You carry the taxes, insurance, and CAM share on the dark space yourself, depending on your other leases' denominators. And you spend your own time marketing, touring, and negotiating.

A renewal skips nearly all of it. No downtime, usually no commission or a reduced one, modest or no improvement dollars, no interruption to the tenant's sales or the center's mix. Even a renewal signed at a modest rent increase — or flat — frequently beats a new lease at a higher face rate once you net out the real costs of the gap. That does not mean renew at any price. It means the burden of proof sits on vacancy, not on retention.

## The 12–18 month runway

The single highest-leverage change an owner can make is starting earlier. Open the renewal conversation 12 to 18 months before expiration.

That window exists for practical reasons. Small retail tenants make stay-or-go decisions slowly: they weigh their lease against their own sales, staffing, and sometimes relocation options that take months to explore. Landlords need lead time too — if the tenant is leaving, you want marketing to begin while the space is still occupied and showing well, not after the lights go dark. And if the answer involves improvements to the space, you need budget runway to plan and price the work.

An early conversation also changes the tone. At 15 months out, "what would make staying an easy decision?" is a partnership question. At 60 days out, the same question sounds like desperation.

## The renewal calendar and option tracking

None of this works from memory. Build a renewal calendar with three entries per lease: the expiration date, the date your early conversation should open (expiration minus 12–18 months), and every option deadline with its notice window.

Options deserve special care because they cut both ways. A tenant's renewal option usually requires written notice by a specific date, often six to twelve months before expiration, and typically locks a pre-set rent or a formula. Track these dates with alarms well in advance. If a tenant misses an option deadline, the option is usually gone — know that before you negotiate as if it still exists. If a tenant holds an option at a rent below today's market, expect them to exercise it, and plan your strategy around the option's terms rather than around a market negotiation. Review the whole calendar monthly. Fifteen minutes, every lease, every month.

## Market rent checks before every conversation

Never enter a renewal conversation without a current view of market rent. Terms vary by market and by submarket, so build your own evidence: recent deals in your center, asking rates at comparable centers nearby, and a periodic call with a local retail broker who actually does deals in your trade area.

The point is not to squeeze the tenant to the top of the range. The point is to know where the range is. If your in-place rent is already at or above market, you walk in knowing the realistic outcome is flat, and you negotiate term, improvements, and timing instead. If your rent is below market, you have room to move toward market over the renewal term — often with stepped increases a good tenant can absorb. What you cannot do is discover which situation you are in after you have already quoted a number.

## The renewal-negotiation preparation checklist

Before you sit down with any tenant, assemble one page with:

- **Payment history.** Months current, late payments, NSF checks, outstanding balances.
- **Lease position.** In-place rent versus your market estimate; remaining term; options and their status; any exclusives or co-tenancy clauses that affect the center.
- **Space condition.** What the suite needs now and what it will need over the next term — HVAC age, storefront, restrooms, electrical.
- **Tenant health signals.** What you can observe: hours kept, staffing, parking in front of their door, any sales figures they report under the lease.
- **Your ask and your floor.** Target rent and escalations, term length, improvement dollars you are willing to invest, and the point where vacancy is the better answer.
- **Their likely ask.** Longer term, lower rate, improvements, or flexibility. Decide your response to each before it comes up.

Preparation is what turns a renewal from an emotional exchange into an arithmetic one.

## Improvements versus rate

When a tenant wants a concession, you have two levers: reduce the rate, or invest in the space. Improvements usually deserve first consideration. Dollars spent on a storefront refresh, a restroom upgrade, or a new HVAC unit stay in your building and add value regardless of who the tenant is. Rate concessions vanish the day the lease ends and reset your comparable rents downward for the next negotiation.

A common structure is to fund an improvement and recover some of its cost through the rent over the term — a higher rate than the tenant wanted, a lower cash cost than a pure concession. Run that amortization openly with the tenant; most respect the math. Reserve rate concessions for tenants whose covenant you genuinely value and whose alternative is a real vacancy risk, and prefer shorter concession periods over lower rates for the whole term.

## When not to renew

Retention is a default, not a rule. Do not renew when the record says otherwise:

- **Chronic late payment or bounced checks.** A renewal extends the collection problem, not the tenancy.
- **A use that no longer fits the mix.** If the tenant's concept has drifted into something that weakens the center's draw or conflicts with exclusives you have granted, vacancy plus re-leasing may be the stronger play.
- **A better use for the space.** Sometimes a suite is worth more to a tenant you have not signed yet. If the space could anchor a remix of that wing, holdover flexibility may beat a long renewal.
- **Terms that destroy the comparable.** A renewal signed far below market to avoid a hard conversation will haunt every future negotiation and your next appraisal.

When you decide not to renew, say so early, in writing where required, and manage the exit professionally. How you end tenancies is part of your reputation with every other tenant watching.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

On a 63,000 SF legacy center like On The Blvd on Arnould Boulevard in Lafayette, retention is the core of the Operator Landlord approach. The operator knows the tenants by name and walks the property weekly, so the 12–18 month conversation is not a cold letter — it is a standing dialogue that simply gets specific about dates and dollars. Legacy centers carry leases written under prior owners, sometimes with option terms nobody has looked at in years. The work is unglamorous: abstract every option and deadline onto one calendar, review it monthly, and treat each renewal as a decision made with evidence rather than a date that arrives by surprise.

## The practical next step

1. Build the renewal calendar: every expiration, every option deadline and notice window, and a conversation-start date 12–18 months ahead of each expiration.
2. Order the next twelve months of expirations and start the earliest conversation this month.
3. Do a market rent check for your center — recent deals, competing asking rates, one broker call — before your next renewal discussion.
4. Draft your one-page preparation sheet and fill it in for the next two renewals on the calendar.
5. For any tenant you would not renew, write down the reason and the plan for the space now, while you still have time to act on it.
