# Deciding When to Sell a Shopping Center

Most shopping centers get sold for one of two reasons: the owner decided to, or events decided for them. The first path produces better outcomes, and the difference is a framework. This article offers one: how to test whether your ownership thesis is complete, how to weigh market timing against personal timing, which capital events force the question, what taxes do to the math at a general level, and the questions worth answering before you call a broker. Nothing here is investment or tax advice; it is a way to structure a decision that is yours to make.

## Selling is a decision, not an event

The owners who sell well tend to share a habit: they decided what would make them sell before anyone asked. They can state, in plain sentences, what the property was supposed to accomplish, what would count as finished, and what would count as a reason to leave early.

Without that frame, the decision gets made by whoever calls first — an unsolicited offer, a broker's market update, a partner's restlessness, a lender's maturity notice. Each of those is real information, but none of them is a reason. The framework below is a way to convert scattered signals into an actual decision, made on your calendar rather than the market's.

A useful starting posture: review the sell-or-hold question deliberately once a year, the way you review insurance or budgets. Deciding not to sell, on purpose, is a decision too — and it is a better one than drifting.

## Test one: is the thesis complete?

Every acquisition implies a thesis, even if it was never written down: buy below replacement cost and re-tenant, fix the operations and refinance, hold for income through the loan term, reposition the front parcel. The first question in any sell-or-hold review is whether the thesis has done its work.

Three states are possible:

- **Complete.** The plan was executed. The vacancy is leased, the systems are in place, the income is stable, and what remains is routine ownership at a stabilized yield. Holding from here is a different decision than the one you originally made — it deserves its own fresh justification.
- **Incomplete but working.** The plan is mid-flight and the evidence is encouraging. Selling now usually means selling the remaining upside to the buyer at a discount. Hold, unless something else has changed.
- **Stalled.** The thesis assumed something that turned out not to be true — a corridor that did not recover, a tenant category that did not materialize, capex that outran the plan. A stalled thesis is not a failure to hide; it is information. Sometimes the right move is to sell to a buyer whose thesis fits the property better than yours now does.

The discipline is honesty about which state you are in. Owners overstate "working" and understate "stalled." A broker's opinion of value, reviewed later, will not rescue a thesis from the second state — but it can tell you what the market currently thinks of the first.

## Market timing versus personal timing

Market timing gets the attention; personal timing does the deciding.

On markets, hedged honesty is the only respectable position. Nobody reliably times commercial real estate cycles, and retail pricing in any given corridor depends on interest rates, lender appetite, buyer sentiment, and local supply — factors that shift and that this article will not pretend to forecast. Terms vary by market, and the difference between a good market and a poor one is often smaller than the difference between a well-prepared property and a poorly prepared one.

What you can actually observe: whether capital is moving in your segment, whether centers like yours are trading, and what a credible broker says buyers are currently underwriting. That is worth checking annually, not daily.

Personal timing is more concrete and often more decisive:

- Is the property still the right size and complexity for your time and attention?
- Does the equity have a better use — another deal, another business, retirement, family needs?
- Are the partners, if any, still aligned on horizon and risk?
- How much of your net worth and your week does this one asset represent?

A complete thesis plus a sound market plus a strong personal reason is a clean exit. Two of three is a judgment call. One of three is usually waiting dressed up as deciding.

## Capital-event triggers

Some events force the sell-or-hold question whether you scheduled it or not. Recognizing them early converts a scramble into a decision.

- **Loan maturity.** A maturing loan requires refinancing or repayment, and refinancing reprices the whole deal at current rates and terms. If the new debt meaningfully changes the return on your equity, the hold case needs re-underwriting — and a sale becomes the natural comparison. Start the analysis twelve to eighteen months before maturity, not ninety days.
- **The capital wall.** Roofs, parking lots, HVAC fleets, and facades arrive on roughly knowable schedules. A major capital event one to three years out is a fork: fund it and hold for the return, or sell and let the buyer price the work. Buyers typically discount deferred maintenance more than the work itself costs, so the honest math matters here.
- **Partner dynamics.** Death, divorce, retirement, disagreement, or a simple divergence of horizons among owners can make sale the cleanest resolution. The time to surface misalignment is early and private, not mid-listing.
- **Concentration and life events.** Health, age, relocation, or a shift in how you want to spend your working hours are legitimate triggers. An asset that demands an operator has a different value to an owner who no longer wants to operate.

The pattern is the same in each case: the event is coming whether or not you plan for it, and planning converts it from pressure into choice.

## Taxes: the part of the math that changes the answer

A sale is not priced by the contract price; it is priced by what you keep. At a general educational level — not tax advice, and your own advisors must run your actual numbers — two ideas shape most shopping center sales.

First, depreciation recapture and capital gains mean the taxable gain on a long-held, depreciated property is often much larger than the owner's intuition suggests. Years of depreciation deductions lower the property's tax basis, so the gain at sale includes recapture that can surprise owners who only compared price to purchase price.

Second, the like-kind exchange under Section 1031 of the Internal Revenue Code allows an owner of investment real estate to defer federal capital gains and recapture taxes by reinvesting proceeds into qualifying replacement property, under strict identification and closing deadlines and typically with a qualified intermediary holding the proceeds. It is a deferral, not an elimination, and the rules are unforgiving — the deadlines are real, and missteps can void the exchange. But for an owner whose next move is another property, it changes the sell-or-hold math enough that it should be modeled before, not after, the decision.

The practical point: bring your CPA into the conversation at the same time as your broker, not after the LOI.

## The questions to answer before calling a broker

Before the first broker conversation, write down answers to these:

1. What did I buy this property to do, and is it done?
2. What is my honest read of the next three years if I hold — income, capex, effort?
3. What are my loan maturity and my next major capital events, and when?
4. What would I do with the proceeds, and what does the tax picture do to the net number?
5. What price or outcome would make me regret selling — and what would make me regret holding?

If you can answer all five in a page, you are ready for a broker conversation to be useful rather than steering. If you cannot, the homework is the next step, not the listing.

On The Blvd — roughly 63,000 SF of legacy multi-tenant retail at 101–149 Arnould Blvd in Lafayette, Louisiana, owned by Belle Realty of Lafayette, LLC and run under the Operator Landlord approach — illustrates why the framework starts with the thesis rather than the market. A center this size rewards active operation, and active operation implies a plan with an endpoint or a deliberate decision to keep going. Whether Belle Realty holds for decades or sells next year, the decision quality comes from the same place: a stated thesis, a calendar of known capital events, and an annual habit of asking the question on purpose.

## The practical next step

1. Write your ownership thesis for the property in five sentences, including what "done" looks like.
2. List the dated events on the horizon: loan maturity, major capex, lease expirations above a set size.
3. Ask your CPA for a preliminary estimate of the tax impact of a sale at a plausible price — before you need it.
4. Set a recurring annual sell-or-hold review, and put it on the calendar.
5. If the review points toward selling, move to preparation: the twelve-month plan in the next article.
