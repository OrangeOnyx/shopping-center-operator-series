# The Retail Due Diligence Checklist: Leases, Estoppels, Title, and Physical Condition

A signed purchase agreement starts a clock, and that clock is short. Most retail contracts allow thirty to sixty days to verify everything the seller has represented about the property. This article lays out a working checklist organized in four phases — documents, financials, physical condition, and third-party reports — so the verification work happens in order and nothing expensive gets missed.

## Due diligence is verification, not discovery

The purpose of due diligence is not to fall in love with the deal. It is to confirm or refute the income story you underwrote. Every representation in the offering memorandum — occupancy, rents, expenses, lease terms — is a claim that needs a document behind it.

A practical approach treats the period as a series of phases with owners and deadlines. Documents first, because they take longest to collect and everything else depends on them. Financials next, because they get tested against the documents. Physical inspection in parallel, because inspectors book out. Third-party reports ordered on day one, because surveys and environmental work have lead times you do not control.

Two habits separate clean diligence from expensive diligence. First, keep a single open-items list — every missing document, every unanswered question, every discrepancy — with a name and a date next to each item. Second, re-run the math at the end. If diligence reveals lower rent, higher expenses, or near-term capital work, the price conversation changes before closing, not after.

## Phase one: The documents

Documents are the foundation. Request them in the first week:

- **Every lease and every amendment**, including assignments, guaranties, and side letters. A rent roll says what the seller believes; the lease says what is enforceable.
- **The current rent roll and twelve months of updates**, so you can see move-ins, move-outs, and delinquency trends rather than a single snapshot.
- **CAM reconciliations for the past three years.** CAM — common area maintenance — is the shared cost of operating the property that most retail tenants reimburse. Reconciliations show whether the seller actually collected what leases allow, or absorbed shortfalls.
- **Estoppel certificates** from every tenant.
- **SNDAs** where existing lenders required them — subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment agreements that govern what happens to a lease if the property changes hands through foreclosure.

### What an estoppel certificate is

An estoppel certificate is a signed statement from a tenant confirming the key facts of its lease: current rent, expiration date, options, security deposit, and whether the landlord owes anything — unpaid tenant improvements, disputed charges, outstanding defaults. Once signed, the tenant is generally barred ("estopped") from later claiming the facts were different.

It matters because the lease file is the seller's version of reality. The estoppel is the tenant's version. When the two disagree — a tenant claims an unrecorded renewal option, or a promised roof repair — you want to know before closing, while the seller still has incentive to fix it. Most lenders require estoppels from major tenants as a funding condition, so start requests early; tenants respond slowly, and leases often give them ten to thirty days.

## Phase two: The financials

With documents in hand, test the numbers:

- **Three years of operating statements**, plus year-to-date. Look for trends in reimbursement income, repairs, and management costs — not just the latest year.
- **Utility bills** for house meters and common areas. Utilities are hard to fudge and often reveal true consumption versus what tenants reimburse.
- **Property tax bills** and the assessment history. In many jurisdictions a sale triggers reassessment; underwrite the post-sale tax number, not the seller's.
- **Bank-verified collections**, where the seller will provide deposit records. Collected rent and billed rent are not the same thing.

The core exercise is reconciliation: tie the rent roll to the leases, tie the operating statements to the rent roll, and tie the expenses to invoices where amounts are material. A center that bills $9.00 per square foot in NNN charges but reconciles at $7.40 collected has told you something important about either its leases or its bookkeeping.

## Phase three: The physical asset

Retail diligence lives in four building systems:

- **Roof.** Age, membrane type, leak history, warranty status, and ponding. Roofs on legacy centers are often patch-on-patch; get a roofing contractor's remaining-life opinion, not just a walk-over.
- **Parking lot.** In many markets the lot is the largest single capital exposure. Look for alligator cracking, drainage failures, and base failure versus surface wear — resurfacing and reconstruction are very different numbers.
- **HVAC.** Count the units, record ages and tonnage, and note which leases make HVAC the tenant's responsibility versus the landlord's. Forty rooftop units at year fifteen is a schedule, not a surprise, if you plan it.
- **Electrical and life safety.** Panel capacity, house lighting, exit signage, and any aluminum branch wiring in older construction.

Add an **ADA accessibility review** of parking, slopes, entrances, and restrooms. Accessibility claims are a real cost category in retail, and the barrier list from a CASp-style or equivalent inspection becomes a budget line rather than a liability surprise.

## Phase four: Third-party reports

Order these in the first week; they gate the closing calendar:

- **Phase I Environmental Site Assessment.** A records-and-reconnaissance study that screens for recognized environmental conditions — prior dry cleaners, fuel uses, nearby spills. A Phase I that flags an issue leads to a Phase II with actual sampling; build time for that possibility.
- **ALTA survey.** Confirms boundaries, easements, encroachments, parking counts, and whether the fenced reality matches the legal description.
- **Appraisal.** Usually a lender requirement, but read it as a second opinion on your underwriting, especially the rent and vacancy assumptions.
- **Title commitment.** The schedule of exceptions is the document that matters — easements, reciprocal easement agreements, and restrictions run with the land and survive your purchase.

Each report has a reviewer. Environmental goes to your attorney and lender. Survey and title go to your attorney together, because exceptions only make sense plotted against the drawing. Read every exception yourself as well; the operator will live with these documents longer than any advisor.

## Where specialists earn their fee

Diligence is one of the places where professional fees are clearly cheaper than mistakes. An acquisitions attorney earns the fee on lease abstracting, estoppel negotiation, and title objections — work where wording decides outcomes. A property inspector earns it on the roof and lot, where a missed reconstruction scope can exceed the entire inspection budget by a factor of ten. An environmental consultant earns it by knowing which prior uses are manageable and which should end the deal.

The operator's job is not to replace these specialists but to direct them: ask the specific question, get the specific answer, and decide. This article is education, not legal advice — every deal should run its document, title, and environmental questions through licensed professionals in the deal's jurisdiction.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

On The Blvd Shopping Center is a legacy multi-tenant center of roughly 63,000 square feet on Arnould Boulevard in Lafayette. For a center of that age and scale, the checklist above is not abstract: the lease file and the estoppels are the income, the roof and the lot are the capital plan, and the CAM reconciliations tell you whether the prior owner ran the recoveries the leases allow. An operator who has lived inside those documents as an owner reads a seller's diligence package differently — faster, and with a shorter list of surprises.

## The practical next step

1. Build your standing diligence checklist now — before you have a deal — with the four phases above and an owner assigned to each.
2. Draft a standard document request list and a standard estoppel form your attorney has approved.
3. Identify your bench in advance: acquisitions attorney, inspector, roofing contractor, environmental consultant, and surveyor, with current pricing and lead times.
4. On your next deal, open the open-items list on day one and review it twice a week until closing.
5. After closing, convert the diligence file into the operating file — leases abstracted, certificates filed, capital list scheduled — so the next buyer inherits a clean package.
