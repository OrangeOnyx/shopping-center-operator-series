# How to Evaluate a Shopping Center Before You Buy

Full due diligence — estoppels, title work, environmental reports, lease audits — comes after you decide a deal deserves it. This article covers the step before that: the first-pass evaluation that tells you, in days rather than months, whether a center warrants the cost of deeper investigation. Four lenses, in order: location, tenancy, physical condition, and the math.

## Location fundamentals

A shopping center's income is downstream of its location, and location cannot be renovated. Evaluate it first.

**The corridor.** What role does this road play in the trade area? Retail corridors have gravity: the side of the street with the grocery anchor, the intersections with the signalized turns, the stretch where the rooftops already are. A center on the wrong side of a strong corridor can underperform a weaker center on the right side. Drive the corridor at morning, lunch, and evening. Count the competing centers and their condition.

**Traffic counts.** Published traffic counts (state DOT and local sources typically provide them) give you daily vehicle volume past the site. Volume alone is not value — commuters racing home are not shoppers — but chronically low counts cap the ceiling for retail that depends on drive-by traffic. Compare the count to the corridor's competing nodes.

**Rooftops.** Retail follows households. Look at population and household counts within one, three, and five miles, and at the trend: growing, stable, or shrinking. Daily-needs retail especially lives or dies on the density of nearby rooftops, because convenience purchases are won or lost within a short drive.

**Access and visibility.** Can a customer see the center, turn into it easily, and get out without a U-turn? Curb cuts, median breaks, signalized entrances, pylon signage visible at driving speed, and sightlines to the storefronts all translate directly into tenant sales — which is to say, into your rent. A center with a hidden entrance or a difficult left turn carries a permanent leasing handicap that rent discounts only partially cure.

## Tenancy and leases

The rent roll is the asset. Look past the top-line number to its structure.

- **Tenant quality.** Who are the tenants, and are their businesses durable? Distinguish credit or established regional operators from thinly capitalized startups — not because small tenants are bad (they are the lifeblood of neighborhood centers), but because turnover risk must be priced. Ask how long each tenant has been in place. Tenure is the market's own vote.
- **Lease terms.** How much term remains, weighted by income? A center whose leases mostly expire within two years is a leasing project, not a stabilized asset. Note options, exclusives, co-tenancy clauses, and any below-market or above-market rents relative to the corridor.
- **The rollover map.** Plot expirations by year against income. Stacked expirations in one year concentrate risk; staggered expirations give you room to work.
- **Recovery structure.** Are the leases NNN, gross, or modified gross — and is the seller actually reconciling and collecting recoveries? Uncollected recoveries are both a warning about management quality and, sometimes, an opportunity.

## Physical condition

You are not yet paying for inspections, so train your eyes. On a first visit, the building tells you how it has been owned.

Roof edges and staining on fascia hint at roof life. Ponding, cracking, and patching patterns in the parking lot tell you whether resurfacing is a budget item or an emergency. Look at HVAC unit ages where visible, signage condition, storefront glass and doors, landscaping, dumpster enclosures, and lighting. Then ask the age and service history of the big items: roof, lot, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, fire systems.

The goal is not a number yet — it is a bucket: light cosmetic work, normal catch-up maintenance, or heavy deferred capital. Each bucket changes what the asking price should mean to you.

## First-pass math

Keep it simple, and keep it honest. Three figures do most of the work. (The numbers below are illustrative only — plug in the deal's actuals.)

**Price per square foot.** Price divided by gross leasable area. If a 63,000 square foot center is offered at $6,300,000, that is $100 per square foot. Compare to replacement cost and to recent same-format sales in the market. It is a sanity check, not a valuation.

**NOI sanity check.** Net operating income is revenue minus operating expenses, before debt service. Rebuild it yourself from the rent roll and an honest expense load rather than accepting the broker's pro forma. Illustratively: if base rents total $840,000, recoveries add $180,000, vacancy and credit loss run 5% of gross, and realistic operating expenses total $300,000, NOI is roughly $669,000 ($840,000 + $180,000 = $1,020,000; less $51,000 vacancy = $969,000; less $300,000 expenses = $669,000). Check the expense ratio against your experience; broker packages notoriously understate management, reserves, and non-recoverable items.

**Cap rate concept.** NOI divided by price is the capitalization rate — the unlevered yield the price implies. At $669,000 NOI on a $7,000,000 ask, the cap rate is about 9.6%. Whether that is attractive depends on market, format, tenancy, and condition — cap rates vary by market and change over time, so benchmark against current local comps rather than any rule of thumb. The point of the first pass is simpler: does the price, the income, and the work required line up with what the market pays for this kind of asset? If not, either negotiate, reprice the work, or walk.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

On The Blvd Shopping Center — approximately 63,000 square feet of multi-tenant legacy retail at 101–149 Arnould Blvd in Lafayette, Louisiana, owned by Belle Realty of Lafayette, LLC — is a useful mental model for what these four lenses resolve into. Location: a center like this sits on an established commercial corridor in Lafayette, where the evaluation questions are exactly the ones above — corridor gravity, traffic, rooftops, access and visibility. Tenancy: a multi-tenant structure means the rent roll is a portfolio of relationships and lease terms to be read one by one, not a single number. Physical condition: a legacy asset has a maintenance history written on its roof and lot, readable in a single careful walk. And the math: at roughly 63,000 square feet, the arithmetic is tractable enough to do on one sheet of paper — which is precisely what a first pass should be. The Operator Landlord lens adds one more question a buyer should ask of any center like this: how has it been operated, and what would active, tenant-partnership ownership recover that passive ownership left on the table?

## The first-visit checklist

Print this and take it to the property:

- [ ] Drive the corridor at three times of day; note competing centers and their condition
- [ ] Record traffic counts from DOT or local sources; note access points, medians, signals
- [ ] Pull population and households within 1, 3, and 5 miles; check the trend
- [ ] Photograph roof edges, lot condition, signage, storefronts, lighting, landscaping, dumpster areas
- [ ] Ask ages and service history: roof, lot, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, fire systems
- [ ] Get the full rent roll with lease terms, options, and expiration dates
- [ ] Map expirations by year against income
- [ ] Identify exclusives, co-tenancy clauses, and anchor rights
- [ ] Ask for trailing twelve-month actuals — not just the pro forma
- [ ] Rebuild NOI yourself; compute price per square foot and cap rate
- [ ] Write down what active operations could improve — and what it would cost

## The practical next step

1. **Build your one-page first-pass template** from the checklist above and use it on every deal, so deals are compared consistently.
2. **Practice on a deal you will not buy.** Run the four lenses on a listed property in your market. Reps make the real evaluation faster and calmer.
3. **Set your walk-away triggers in advance** — for example, maximum stacked rollover, minimum corridor traffic, capital bucket you will not touch. Deciding standards before emotions arrive is the whole game.
4. **Line up your diligence team before you need it:** a real estate attorney, a lender, an inspector or roofer you trust, and a broker who knows the format.
5. **Keep a deal log.** Every center you evaluate, with your first-pass numbers and your decision. In two years it will be your best market intelligence.
