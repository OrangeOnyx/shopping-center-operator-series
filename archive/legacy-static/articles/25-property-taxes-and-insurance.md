# Property Taxes and Insurance: Managing the Costs You Don't Control

Base rent you negotiate. Vendor contracts you bid. But two of the largest expense lines on a shopping center's P&L arrive from outside your control: the tax assessor sets the assessment, and the insurance market sets the premium. What remains fully within your control is how prepared you are — the calendar discipline, the documentation, and the program design that determine whether these costs are managed or merely endured. This article covers both, in general educational terms. It is not legal, tax, or insurance advice; rules vary by jurisdiction, and your own professionals should guide your specific decisions.

## Property taxes: assessed value vs. market value

The first discipline is understanding what you're actually being taxed on. The **assessed value** is the figure the taxing authority assigns for tax purposes; the **market value** is what a buyer would pay. The two are related but not identical — assessment ratios, valuation methods, and timing all create gaps between them, and the details differ by jurisdiction.

For an income-producing property like a shopping center, assessment often involves some form of income analysis: the assessor estimates the property's income stream and converts it to value. That matters to you for one practical reason: **the assessor's income assumptions may not match your property's reality.** A center carrying vacancy, collecting below-assumed rents, or burdened by unusual expenses may be valued on an income stream it doesn't actually produce. The gap between the assessor's assumptions and your actuals is where appeals are won.

Keep three habits:

- **Read the assessment notice the day it arrives.** Notices carry deadlines, and the window to respond is often short.
- **Reconcile the assessor's assumptions.** If the valuation implies rents, occupancy, or expense ratios that don't match your books, that's your evidence file.
- **Track the ratio.** Assessed value relative to your own estimate of market value, year over year, tells you whether the assessment is drifting.

## The protest cycle: calendar discipline wins

Assessment appeals are process work. The operator who wins them is usually not the one with the cleverest argument but the one who didn't miss a deadline. Practices and terminology vary by jurisdiction — the following is general and hedged, not advice for any specific parcel.

In Louisiana, for example, property is administered at the **parish** level rather than the county level, and assessments follow cycles set by state law, with parish assessors updating values on a periodic reassessment schedule and notices going out on published calendars. Across the Gulf South generally, appeal windows are typically measured in weeks, not months, and often run from the date assessment rolls open for public inspection or notices are mailed — not from when you happen to read them.

A workable calendar discipline looks like this:

1. **Know your parish's (or county's) cycle.** When are values set, when do rolls open, when is the appeal deadline? Put all three dates in the recurring-operations calendar with reminders well in advance.
2. **Assemble the evidence file before you need it.** Current rent roll, trailing P&L showing actual vacancy and expenses, photos of deferred condition, and any relevant comparable assessments. If the property's income is the argument, your own clean books are the exhibit.
3. **Decide the escalation path in advance.** Many jurisdictions offer informal review with the assessor's office before formal board appeals, with further review available beyond that. Knowing the sequence — and each level's deadline — keeps you from improvising under time pressure.
4. **Consider professional help proportionally.** Tax consultants and attorneys who work on contingency or flat fee can make sense for larger assessments; for smaller ones, a well-documented informal review may be enough. Evaluate cost against the dollars at stake.

One boundary worth respecting: don't protest reflexively every year with no case. Assessors' offices are staffed by professionals doing recurring business, and credibility is an asset. Bring the appeals where the evidence supports them.

## Insurance: designing the program

Insurance for a retail center is not one policy but a program. The major components:

- **Property coverage.** Replacement cost of the buildings and improvements, typically on a special-perils or equivalent form. The central question is the **valuation**: insure to accurate replacement cost, not to market value and not to a stale figure from five years ago. Construction cost inflation has been significant in recent years; an outdated valuation can leave you underinsured and exposed to coinsurance penalties. Have the replacement cost estimate refreshed periodically.
- **General liability.** Covers the slip-and-fall world of common areas. Limits and structure should reflect the actual traffic and tenant mix; your broker and counsel can benchmark what's customary for your market.
- **Flood.** In South Louisiana this deserves its own paragraph's worth of attention, even in general terms. Standard property policies typically exclude flood. Coverage may be available through the National Flood Insurance Program or private flood markets, and whether to carry it — and at what limits — depends on the property's flood zone, elevation, and your tolerance for a loss that is not theoretical in this region. This is a conversation to have explicitly with your broker, not an assumption to make by default.
- **Wind and hail.** In Gulf South markets, wind and named-storm coverage often carries a **separate, percentage-based deductible** — commonly a percentage of the insured value rather than a flat dollar amount. On an illustrative building insured for $8 million, a 2% wind deductible means $160,000 out of pocket before coverage responds. That number belongs in your operating-reserve planning, not just your policy file.

Two design levers deserve deliberate choice: **deductibles** (higher deductibles lower premiums but raise the cash you must hold in reserve) and **umbrella/excess liability limits** (relatively inexpensive protection against severe claims). Both are tradeoffs; state them explicitly in the budget.

## Broker relationships and renewal discipline

Treat your insurance broker like a key vendor — which means managing the relationship rather than accepting it:

- **Start renewals 90–120 days out.** Gulf South property markets can be tight; late submissions get late, expensive answers.
- **Ask what markets were approached** and why terms changed. A broker who can explain the placement is doing the job; one who delivers a number without context is not.
- **Benchmark periodically.** Loyalty has value, but so does competition. Every few years, have a second broker review the program — coverage gaps and stale valuations surface quickly under fresh eyes.

## Documenting the property for underwriting

Underwriters price what they can see. A well-documented property gets better terms than a mysterious one, and the documentation overlaps heavily with what your operating system already keeps:

- **Current replacement-cost valuation** and building specifications (construction type, roof age and material, electrical and plumbing updates).
- **Renovation and maintenance records.** A documented new roof or updated electrical panel is premium-relevant evidence; an undocumented one doesn't exist.
- **Loss history with context.** If you've had claims, document the remediation. Underwriters fear patterns, not single events with clean fixes.
- **Life-safety and risk features.** Lighting, cameras, fire protection, and maintenance cadence all speak to how the property is run.

The same file serves the tax appeal, the refinance, and the eventual sale. Build it once; use it four times.

## The practical next step

1. **Find your assessment calendar.** Confirm your parish's (or county's) reassessment cycle and appeal deadlines, and put them in the recurring calendar with 30-day advance reminders.
2. **Open the evidence file.** Current rent roll, trailing P&L, and property photos — assembled now, not when the notice arrives.
3. **Pull your policies.** Confirm the property valuation date, the wind/hail deductible structure, and whether flood coverage exists. List the gaps you find.
4. **Set the renewal clock.** Ninety to 120 days before expiration, calendar the renewal kickoff with your broker.
5. **Match reserves to deductibles.** Confirm your operating reserve covers at least your largest single deductible exposure.
