# CAM and NNN Reconciliation: Retail Leasing's Most Misunderstood Work

In a triple-net shopping center, the tenant pays base rent plus a share of the property's operating costs. Every month the tenant pays an estimate. Once a year, the landlord settles the difference between estimates and actuals. That settlement — the reconciliation — is routine accounting, but it is also the moment tenants decide whether the landlord's numbers can be trusted. This article walks through what gets passed through, how shares are calculated, and how to run a clean annual reconciliation step by step.

## What CAM and NNN actually pass through

CAM stands for common area maintenance: the cost of operating the parts of the center that every tenant shares. NNN — triple net — means the tenant reimburses the landlord for three nets on top of base rent: property taxes, insurance, and CAM. In a gross lease, by contrast, the landlord absorbs those costs inside a higher rent.

A typical CAM pool includes:

- Parking lot sweeping, striping touch-ups, and asphalt patching
- Landscaping, irrigation, and seasonal plantings
- Common area electricity, including parking lot lighting
- Common area water and sewer
- Trash service and pest control for shared areas
- Security, if the center provides it
- A management fee, where the lease permits one

What stays out matters as much as what goes in. Most leases exclude capital expenditures, leasing commissions, the landlord's income taxes, debt service, and costs of correcting the landlord's default. National tenants negotiate exclusion lists line by line. Local tenants often sign the landlord's form as written. Either way, the lease — not the landlord's bookkeeping habits — defines the pool. Before reconciling anything, read each lease's definition of CAM. In a legacy center with leases signed across different eras, you may find three or four different definitions in force at once.

## Pro-rata share, caps, and gross-ups

Three mechanisms shape what any individual tenant owes.

**Pro-rata share.** The tenant's square footage divided by the center's leasable square footage — or, in some leases, the square footage actually leased and occupied. That denominator question is not trivia. On a partially vacant center, a "leased" denominator pushes a bigger share onto occupied tenants; a "leasable" denominator leaves the landlord carrying the vacant space's share. Know which standard each lease uses.

**Caps.** Many leases cap "controllable" expenses — the CAM categories the landlord can influence, typically excluding taxes, insurance, and utilities. Caps come in two flavors: cumulative (the cap base grows by a fixed percentage each year from a base year) and non-cumulative (each year's pool cannot exceed the prior year's actual by more than the cap percentage). A 5% cumulative cap compounds; a 5% non-cumulative cap does not. Ten years in, that difference is real money.

**Gross-ups.** When occupancy is low, variable expenses like janitorial or utilities are lower than they would be at full occupancy. A gross-up clause lets the landlord adjust variable expenses to what they would be at a stated occupancy level — often 95% or 100% — before applying pro-rata shares. The logic is fairness both ways: tenants should not pay for vacancy they did not cause, and the landlord should not absorb costs that scale with occupancy. Gross-ups only apply where the lease says so, and usually only to variable expenses, not fixed ones like landscaping contracts.

## Estimates vs. actuals: the monthly rhythm

Reconciliation starts long before year-end. At the start of each year, the landlord sets a CAM and NNN budget and bills each tenant a monthly estimate — one-twelfth of the projected annual share. Good estimates do two jobs: they smooth the tenant's cash flow, and they keep the landlord from lending tenants money all year.

Set estimates from the actual budget, not from last year's actuals plus a guess. If you know the insurance renewal came in 12% higher, the estimates should reflect that in January, not as a surprise the following spring. When a mid-year cost shock hits — a major lot repair, a tax reassessment — many leases allow a mid-year estimate adjustment with notice. Use that right carefully, but use it.

## The annual reconciliation, step by step

Here is the process as an operator should run it.

1. **Close the books.** Finalize the year's actual expenses by category, with invoices and vendor records filed. No reconciliation is better than the ledger underneath it.
2. **Sort expenses against lease definitions.** Assign each cost to CAM, taxes, insurance, or landlord-only. Remove excluded items. This is where legacy centers earn their reputation for messy pools — a roof replacement coded into CAM will be found, and it should be.
3. **Apply gross-ups and caps per tenant.** These are tenant-specific calculations. Build a schedule per lease, not one blended number for the center.
4. **Compute each tenant's share.** Square footage times the denominator the lease specifies, applied to each reconciled pool.
5. **Compare to estimates billed.** The difference is a balance due or a credit.
6. **Produce the statement.** One page per tenant: pool totals, their share percentage, their share in dollars, estimates paid, and the settlement amount. Attach the expense summary the lease requires.
7. **Deliver inside the lease window.** Many leases require reconciliation statements within 90 to 180 days of year-end. Missing the window can waive the landlord's right to collect.
8. **Collect or credit, and log it.** Apply credits against the next rent or cut a check per the lease. Record delivery dates and responses.

**Illustrative example** (all figures hypothetical): Imagine a 63,000 SF neighborhood center with a CAM pool of $210,000 for the year, taxes of $95,000, and insurance of $40,000. A tenant occupying 3,500 SF has a 5% pro-rata share on a leasable denominator, with no applicable cap. Their actual share is $10,500 of CAM, $4,750 of taxes, and $2,000 of insurance — $17,250 total. If they paid estimates of $1,300 per month ($15,600 for the year), the reconciliation bills a balance of $1,650. If they had paid $1,550 per month, the statement carries a $1,350 credit. Same lease, same math, opposite direction. Tenants accept both when the arithmetic is visible.

## Audit rights and disputes

Most leases give tenants the right to audit the reconciliation, typically within 12 to 24 months of receiving the statement, sometimes with conditions: the tenant must be current on rent, the audit must be by a qualified accountant, contingency-fee auditors may be barred. Treat an audit notice as a records request, not an attack. Provide the ledger detail the lease requires, correct genuine errors promptly, and document the resolution in writing.

Most disputes are not fraud claims. They are category arguments: whether the management fee was capped correctly, whether a repair was capital, whether the gross-up was applied to a fixed expense. Clean reconciliations settle these quickly because the statement already shows the logic. Dirty ones invite tenants to assume the worst about everything else you bill.

There is a lender dimension too. When you refinance or sell, the buyer's or lender's underwriting will test whether recoveries match the leases. A history of on-time, documented reconciliations — with few disputes and settled audits — reads as management quality. A shoebox of unreconciled years reads as risk, and risk gets priced.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

At a 63,000 SF legacy center like On The Blvd on Arnould Boulevard in Lafayette, reconciliation is personal. The tenants are businesses the operator sees every week. Under the Operator Landlord approach, the annual statement is not a bill that arrives from a faceless manager; it is a report from a partner. That means delivering it on time, walking through it with any tenant who asks, and fixing errors without making the tenant chase. Legacy centers often carry leases written under prior ownership with different CAM definitions, caps, and denominators. The discipline is building a lease-by-lease abstract so each tenant is reconciled against their own document — not against whatever the spreadsheet assumed.

## The practical next step

1. Pull every lease and build a one-page abstract per tenant: CAM definition, denominator, caps, gross-up rights, exclusions, reconciliation deadline, and audit window.
2. Set next year's estimates from the actual budget, and calendar the delivery deadline for each lease's reconciliation statement.
3. Reconcile this year before the earliest lease deadline, even if your habit has been to batch them later.
4. Create a standard statement template that shows pool totals, share math, estimates paid, and settlement on one page.
5. File each year's reconciliation with the invoices behind it, indexed so an audit request can be answered in days, not weeks.
