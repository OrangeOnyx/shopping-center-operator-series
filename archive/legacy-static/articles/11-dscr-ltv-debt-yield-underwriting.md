# DSCR, LTV, and Debt Yield: How Lenders Actually Underwrite Retail

Owners walk into a financing conversation thinking about the loan they want. Lenders start somewhere else: three tests, run against the property's income and value, that produce three different maximum loan amounts. The loan you get is the smallest of the three. This article shows you how to compute each test on your own center, with clearly labeled illustrative numbers, so you can size a deal the way the credit committee will.

## The three gates every retail loan must pass

A retail mortgage has to survive three different failure modes, and lenders built one ratio for each.

- **Cash flow failure.** The center stops generating enough income to make the payment. The test is the debt service coverage ratio, or DSCR.
- **Collateral failure.** The center's value falls below the loan balance. The test is loan-to-value, or LTV.
- **Refinancing failure.** At maturity, the loan cannot be refinanced out of the property's income. The test is debt yield.

Each test produces a ceiling. The lender sizes the loan to the most conservative ceiling, then layers on adjustments for retail-specific risk. If you know your own numbers, you know your loan size within a narrow band before you ever fill out an application. If you don't, the lender's term sheet will feel arbitrary. It isn't.

## NOI first: the number everything divides from

Every underwriting ratio starts with net operating income. NOI is the property's effective gross income — rent actually collected, plus expense reimbursements, minus vacancy and credit loss — less operating expenses like taxes, insurance, common area maintenance, management, and repairs. NOI excludes debt service, depreciation, income taxes, and usually capital expenditures, though many lenders deduct a replacement reserve anyway.

An illustrative example: imagine a neighborhood center with $780,000 of gross potential rent, 8% vacancy and collection loss, $65,000 of other income, and $235,000 of operating expenses. Effective gross income is $782,400; NOI is $547,400. Round numbers matter less than the method.

Here is the part owners underestimate: the lender rarely uses your NOI as-is. Underwriting typically normalizes it. A lender may insert a market management fee even if you self-manage, add reserves of $0.15–$0.25 per square foot, smooth out one-time income, and haircut expense reimbursements that depend on a shaky tenant. If leases roll soon, some lenders stress NOI downward to reflect downtime and re-leasing costs. Your $547,400 can become $500,000 in the lender's model before any ratio is computed. Underwrite yourself the same way, or the math below will flatter you.

## The coverage tests: DSCR and LTV

### Debt service coverage ratio

DSCR equals NOI divided by annual debt service. It answers: how many times does the center's income cover the mortgage payment? For retail, lenders typically look for a minimum of 1.20x to 1.35x, with the requirement rising for weaker tenancy or heavy near-term rollover. Terms vary by lender and market.

To size a loan from DSCR, divide NOI by the required coverage to get maximum annual debt service, then convert that payment to a loan amount using the loan constant — the annual debt service per dollar borrowed at a given rate and amortization.

Illustrative: NOI of $500,000 with a 1.25x requirement allows $400,000 of annual debt service. At a 6.75% rate with a 25-year amortization, the annual constant is roughly 8.3%, so the maximum loan is about $4.8 million.

### Loan-to-value

LTV equals the loan amount divided by appraised value. For stabilized retail, lenders commonly cap LTV between 65% and 75%, depending on asset quality, tenancy, and lender type. Value is usually NOI divided by a market cap rate.

Illustrative: $500,000 of NOI at a 6.5% cap rate implies a value near $7.7 million. A 65% LTV ceiling allows a $5.0 million loan; a 75% ceiling allows $5.8 million.

## Debt yield and how the lender sizes the loan

Debt yield equals NOI divided by the loan amount, expressed as a percentage. Think of it as the lender's cap rate on its own loan: if the lender foreclosed on day one, what yield would the property produce against the loan balance? Lenders typically look for minimum debt yields around 9% to 10% or higher for retail, and the metric has become the quiet favorite because it ignores interest rates, amortization games, and optimistic appraisals. Max loan equals NOI divided by the minimum debt yield.

Illustrative: $500,000 of NOI at a 10% minimum debt yield caps the loan at $5.0 million.

Now put the three tests side by side for the same illustrative center:

| Test | Lender requirement (illustrative) | Maximum loan |
|---|---|---|
| DSCR | 1.25x; 6.75% rate, 25-yr am (≈8.3% constant) | ≈ $4.8 million |
| LTV | 65% of $7.7 million appraised value | ≈ $5.0 million |
| Debt yield | 10.0% minimum | ≈ $5.0 million |

The lender lends $4.8 million — the DSCR constraint binds. Change one input and the binding constraint moves: at a lower rate, DSCR loosens and debt yield takes over; at a soft appraisal, LTV binds. Sophisticated borrowers run this table under three NOI scenarios — in-place, lender-normalized, and stressed — because the lender is running all three.

## What makes retail underwriting different

Retail income is lumpy in ways multifamily and industrial income is not, and underwriting reflects it.

- **Rollover concentration.** If 40% of your rent expires within 24 months, expect a NOI haircut or a lower proceeds quote. Lenders track weighted average lease term, or WALT, and treat a short WALT as a refinance risk, not just a leasing task.
- **Tenant credit.** A national credit tenant supports full credit for its rent. A local operator with thin financials may get its income discounted or excluded from stressed scenarios.
- **Dark boxes and go-dark risk.** A lease that lets an anchor stop operating while paying reduced or full rent still alarms underwriters, because a dark anchor drags the whole center's sales and small-shop renewal odds.
- **Co-tenancy clauses.** If small-shop rents drop when an anchor closes, lenders model that drop whether or not you expect it.
- **Percentage rent and reimbursements.** Variable income gets averaged, discounted, or ignored. Only durable income gets full credit.

## Where self-underwriting goes wrong

Owners running these numbers for the first time tend to make the same handful of errors, all in the optimistic direction.

- **Using scheduled rent instead of collected rent.** Underwrite effective income — what actually hit the bank after vacancy, concessions, and credit loss — not the rent roll's face value.
- **Skipping the management fee and reserves.** If you self-manage, the lender still prices management at market, commonly 3–5% of revenue, and deducts replacement reserves. Include both in your own model.
- **Anchoring on your basis.** Your purchase price and your tax assessment are not value. Lenders use current market value from an appraisal, which moves with cap rates and comparable sales whether you like it or not.
- **Ignoring rollover.** Income from a lease expiring in nine months is worth less than income from a lease with five years left. Stress it before the lender does.
- **Running one scenario.** A single point estimate is a hope, not an underwriting. Run in-place, normalized, and stressed NOI through all three ratios and look at the worst result. That number is closer to what a credit committee will actually approve.

The habit to build: underwrite your own center once a quarter, as if you were the lender. Owners who know their binding constraint negotiate from facts.

## The practical next step

1. Compute your own DSCR, LTV, and debt yield this week using trailing-12-month NOI, a realistic market cap rate, and a current rate quote.
2. Build the three-constraint sizing table above for your property, then rerun it with NOI stressed down 10%.
3. Calculate your WALT and flag every lease expiring within 24 months — that is the list your lender will stress first.
4. Normalize your NOI the way a lender would: add a management fee, deduct reserves, and strip one-time income.
5. Bring your own table to the first lender meeting. It reframes the conversation from "what will you give me" to "here is what the asset supports."
