# Zoning, Environmental, and Title Risks in Retail Acquisitions

Most acquisition analysis focuses on income. But three quieter categories decide what the property can legally be and do over the long term: zoning, environmental condition, and title. These risks rarely show up in the rent roll. They live in ordinances, historical records, and recorded documents, and they transfer to the buyer at closing whether or not anyone looked. This article walks through each category as an education in what to check and when to bring in professionals. It is not legal advice — these are exactly the areas where licensed counsel earns the fee.

## Zoning: what the law lets the center be

Start with a **zoning verification letter** from the local planning authority — a written confirmation of the property's zoning classification, permitted uses, and compliance status. Do not rely on the seller's word or a listing sheet. The letter is the baseline document lenders and attorneys will want in the file.

Then test three specific exposures:

- **Nonconforming use risk.** Many legacy centers were built under older codes. If the current configuration — density, setbacks, building placement — no longer matches the zoning ordinance, the center may be "legal nonconforming." That status usually lets you keep operating as-is, but it can restrict expansion, redevelopment, and sometimes rebuilding after casualty. Ask directly: if this center burned tomorrow, could it be rebuilt as it stands?
- **Parking ratios.** Retail zoning typically requires a set number of spaces per 1,000 square feet of floor area. A center that is tight on parking has a hidden ceiling on tenant mix — restaurants and medical users consume parking fast, and a ratio that works for soft goods may not support the restaurant pad you want to add. Count the actual striped spaces against the requirement.
- **Signage rights.** Sign ordinances cap height, area, and sometimes the number of signs. For a retail center, the pylon sign is a leasing asset. Confirm what exists is permitted, and what a new tenant could legally add.

Zoning questions belong to a land-use attorney or planner when anything comes back unclear. The cost of a verification letter and an hour of counsel is trivial next to discovering a nonconforming structure after closing.

## Environmental: the history under the slab

Retail environmental risk is mostly about **prior uses**. The screening tool is the Phase I Environmental Site Assessment: a records review, government database search, and site reconnaissance that looks for "recognized environmental conditions." If the Phase I flags something, a Phase II follows — soil and groundwater sampling that turns suspicion into fact.

Two prior uses deserve special attention in retail:

- **Dry cleaners.** Historic dry cleaning operations used solvents, particularly perchloroethylene, that persist in soil and groundwater for decades. A cleaner that operated in the 1970s or 1980s — even in a suite now occupied by a sandwich shop — is one of the most common environmental findings in older shopping centers. Cleanup can be manageable under state voluntary programs, but it must be priced and understood before closing.
- **Gas stations and auto uses.** Former fuel service, even on an outparcel or an adjacent lot, means potential underground tanks and petroleum impacts. Ask where tanks were, whether they were removed, and whether closure letters exist.

Older construction adds a third category: **asbestos**. Roofing felts, floor tile, mastic, and pipe insulation in pre-1980 buildings frequently contain asbestos, which becomes an expensive problem at renovation or demolition rather than during ordinary operations. A pre-acquisition survey of materials you plan to disturb converts a future surprise into a budget line.

Environmental findings do not always kill deals. They reprice them, restructure them — escrow, indemnity, insurance — or occasionally end them, which is also a good outcome if the alternative was owning the problem. Route every Phase I through your attorney and lender, and let the environmental consultant, not optimism, decide whether a Phase II is warranted.

## Title: the promises that run with the land

Title work tells you what you are actually buying and what obligations come attached. The title commitment's schedule of exceptions is the operative document, and in retail it usually contains four things that matter:

- **Easements.** Utility easements, drainage servitudes, and shared-drive easements are routine. What you are checking is location and scope — an easement through the middle of the parking field is different from one along the rear boundary.
- **REAs and CC&Rs.** Reciprocal easement agreements and declarations of covenants bind the center and often its anchors and outparcels into shared obligations: common-area maintenance, parking, construction standards, sometimes use restrictions and exclusives. These documents can limit what you may lease — an anchor's exclusive on pharmacies or groceries can block a tenant you assumed was available. Read every restriction with the leasing plan in mind.
- **Access agreements.** Cross-access with neighboring parcels keeps traffic flowing but also binds you to maintain shared drives. Confirm the access points you observed physically match the recorded rights.
- **Outparcels.** Parcels within or along the center that you do not own — often restaurant or bank pads — typically have their own rights under the REA: access, parking allocations, approval rights over changes. Know what the outparcel owners can say no to.

Every exception should be plotted on the survey and read by your attorney. The operator should read them too — you will live under these documents for the entire hold.

## Reprice, restructure, or walk

Findings in these three categories call for one of three responses, and choosing among them is a business decision made with professional advice.

**Reprice** when the risk is quantifiable and bounded. An asbestos survey that prices out at a known removal cost, or a parking shortfall that limits but does not block the leasing plan, belongs in the price negotiation as a credit or reduction.

**Restructure** when the risk is real but manageable through the deal itself. Environmental findings frequently land here: seller-funded remediation before closing, an escrow held back until closure letters arrive, environmental insurance, or an indemnity from a seller with the balance sheet to stand behind it. Title issues sometimes restructure as well — an easement relocated, an outparcel owner's consent obtained as a closing condition.

**Walk** when the risk is unbounded or the seller will not engage. A nonconforming structure that cannot be rebuilt after casualty, in a market where that matters to your plan. A solvent plume with no responsible party and no workable state program. A recorded exclusive that blocks the tenant mix your entire thesis depends on. Walking away costs the diligence spend; staying costs the asset. Experienced buyers treat a disciplined walk as a successful outcome.

The discipline is deciding the response framework before emotions attach to the deal — and letting your attorney, consultant, and lender see findings as they arrive, not after you have rationalized them.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

On The Blvd Shopping Center is a legacy multi-tenant asset of roughly 63,000 square feet in Lafayette. Centers of that vintage carry exactly this profile: zoning written for an earlier era of retail, decades of tenant history under the slab, and recorded documents layered over decades of ownership. The Operator Landlord approach treats zoning status, environmental history, and title exceptions as permanent entries in the property's operating file — reviewed when the leasing plan changes, not only at acquisition. The goal is not to eliminate these risks; it is to know precisely which ones the center carries.

## The practical next step

1. Add three items to your standing diligence checklist: zoning verification letter, Phase I ESA with historical-use review, and full title-exception plot on the survey.
2. Ask specifically about dry cleaning, fuel, and auto-service history — at the property and on adjacent parcels.
3. Read every use restriction and exclusive in the REA or CC&Rs against your intended leasing plan.
4. Confirm parking counts and ratios before promising space to parking-intensive uses like restaurants or medical.
5. Engage local land-use and title counsel early — the jurisdictions differ, and this is their home field.
