# Preventive Maintenance and Capital Planning for Aging Retail Assets

Every legacy shopping center carries a hidden schedule. The roof has years left, not decades. The parking lot is quietly oxidizing. The HVAC fleet is aging in place, one compressor at a time. Owners who treat these as surprises experience them as crises — emergency assessments, deferred refinancing, a buyer's discount at sale. Owners who treat them as a schedule experience them as budget line items. Capital planning is the discipline of converting the physical decline of a building into a funded, sequenced plan. This article lays out how.

## Repairs versus capital: draw the line on purpose

Start with the distinction, because everything downstream depends on it. Operating repairs restore an existing asset to working condition: patching a roof leak, replacing a contactor on an HVAC unit, filling potholes, repainting a storefront beam. Capital expenditures replace or substantially extend the life of a system: a new roof, a parking lot overlay, a full HVAC unit replacement, a façade renovation.

The line matters for three reasons. First, taxes: repairs and capital improvements are generally treated differently, and your accountant needs them classified consistently — the details are their job, but the classification starts in your books. Second, leases: most NNN leases exclude capital items from CAM or allow them only amortized, so miscoding capital work into the operating pool invites reconciliation disputes. Third, planning: repairs belong in the operating budget; capital belongs in a multi-year plan funded by reserves. An owner who lets the two blend discovers the true cost of the building only when something fails.

## The property condition assessment as the backbone

You cannot plan what you have not measured. The backbone of a capital plan is a property condition assessment — PCA — a systematic inspection of the building's major systems by a qualified professional: roof structure and membrane, parking lot pavement and drainage, HVAC equipment age and condition, façade and storefront systems, signage, lighting, plumbing and electrical infrastructure, and life-safety items.

A good PCA does two things: it estimates remaining useful life for each system, and it prices probable replacement or major repair costs in today's dollars. Lenders order PCAs during underwriting for a reason — the document converts a building's condition into a schedule of future costs. An owner should want the same document for the same reason, whether or not a lender is asking.

For an aging center, commission a PCA if you have never had one, and refresh it periodically or update it internally as work is completed. Between formal assessments, your own vendors supply the data: the roofer's annual inspection notes, the HVAC contractor's unit-by-unit condition report, the paving contractor's read on your lot's remaining life. Ask for these in writing. They are free inputs to the plan.

## Building the multi-year capital plan

With condition data in hand, build the plan as a simple table: one row per system, columns for remaining life, estimated cost, and the year the work should land. For a typical aging neighborhood center, the core systems are:

- **Roof.** Inspections annually; budget for recoating or replacement at end of membrane life. Roof work is the most expensive surprise in retail, and the most preventable.
- **Parking lot.** A layered program: crack sealing and patching annually, sealcoat and restripe every few years, mill-and-overlay when the base allows, full reconstruction when it does not. Deferred pavement work compounds faster than almost anything else on the site.
- **HVAC fleet.** Track every rooftop unit by age and condition. Replace units at end of life on a planned rotation rather than in midsummer emergencies at emergency prices.
- **Façade and storefronts.** Paint, stucco or masonry repair, canopy and fascia work — the visible age of the center, and the first thing tenants, customers, and appraisers read.
- **Signage.** Pylon and monument sign refurbishment, panel replacement, lighting conversion.
- **Lighting.** Parking lot and common area lighting, including LED conversion, which often pays for itself through energy and maintenance savings over time.

Sequence the plan by urgency first — life safety and water intrusion always jump the queue — then by economics, then by visibility. A ten-year horizon with the first three years detailed is enough. The plan is a living document: update it every year at budget time and every time a project completes or a system surprises you.

## Reserve funding math

A plan without funding is a wish list. The funding mechanism is the capital reserve: money set aside from operations every month, before it can be spent on anything else.

**Illustrative example** (all figures hypothetical): Imagine a 70,000 SF neighborhood center whose capital plan shows the following over the next ten years: roof replacement at $280,000 in year 6, parking lot overlay and restripe at $190,000 in year 3, sealcoating cycles of $18,000 in years 1, 4, 7, and 10, HVAC replacements of $12,000 per year on average, façade work at $60,000 in year 5, and signage and lighting upgrades of $45,000 in year 2. The ten-year total is roughly $793,000, or about $79,300 per year — call it $6,600 per month of reserve funding.

Now the tradeoff becomes visible. If the center's cash flow supports $4,000 per month of reserves, the plan must stretch, the cheaper scopes must be evaluated, or the owner accepts accumulating deferred maintenance and its consequences at refinance or sale. None of those answers is wrong — but the reserve math forces the choice into the open, in advance, instead of at the worst possible moment. Set the reserve up as a separate account with automatic monthly transfers. Money that stays in the operating account gets spent.

## How capital planning feeds refinancing and sale narratives

Capital planning is not only an operating tool. It is a valuation tool.

At refinancing, lenders underwrite both the income and the asset. An owner who arrives with a current PCA, a multi-year capital plan, and a funded reserve history is presenting a managed risk. Lenders typically look for exactly this evidence that known capital needs are known — and budgeted. The conversation shifts from "what is wrong with this building?" to "here is the schedule and the funding."

At sale, the effect is larger. Buyers price uncertainty aggressively. Every unexplained crack in the lot and every twenty-year-old rooftop unit becomes a discount, usually a generous one in the buyer's favor. A documented capital plan with completed work recorded — invoices, dates, warranties — converts uncertainty into a schedule the buyer can underwrite. Some owners accelerate visible capital items in the years before a sale for precisely this reason: a new roof and a freshly overlaid lot do not just remove objections; they change what the buyer's inspector can find.

The narrative is the same in both rooms: this asset is known, its needs are scheduled, and its operator runs it deliberately. That narrative is worth real dollars, and it costs only the discipline you should be practicing anyway.

### The view from Arnold Blvd

A 70,000 SF legacy center like On The Blvd on Arnold Boulevard in Lafayette is exactly the asset class this discipline serves. Decades of Gulf Coast sun, rain, and storm seasons have written their schedule into the roof, the lot, and the mechanical systems whether anyone has written it down or not. The Operator Landlord approach means writing it down: knowing each major system's condition and remaining life, funding the reserve before the month consumes the cash, and choosing the sequence of roof, lot, and façade work instead of having the sequence chosen by failure. Legacy does not mean neglected. It means the capital plan is not optional — it is the operating strategy.

## The practical next step

1. Inventory your major systems — roof, lot, HVAC units, façade, signage, lighting — with age and last major work for each. If you cannot fill in the table, commission a PCA.
2. Separate repairs from capital in your bookkeeping going forward, and reclassify the current year while it is still clean.
3. Draft a ten-year capital plan with costs in today's dollars, detailed for the first three years.
4. Compute the monthly reserve your plan requires, open a separate account, and automate the transfer — even if the starting number is partial.
5. Ask your roofer, paving contractor, and HVAC vendor for written condition assessments at their next visits, and file them with the plan.
