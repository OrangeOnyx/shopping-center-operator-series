# Vendor Management: Roofs, Lots, Landscaping, and HVAC

A shopping center owner does not maintain a property so much as manage the people who do. Roofs, parking lots, landscaping, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, pest control, signage — nearly every physical system in the center is touched by a contractor. The quality of your vendor bench shows up in tenant complaints, in the CAM pool, and eventually in the appraisal. Vendor management is not glamorous work, but it is where an operator's standards become the property's condition. Here is how to run it deliberately.

## Build the bench before you need it

The worst time to find a roofer is when water is coming through the ceiling. Build the bench in calm weather: at least two qualified vendors for every critical trade — roofing, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, paving and striping, landscaping, sweeping, pest control, and a general handyman for the everything-else category.

Sources are straightforward. Other local owners and brokers will share names if you ask. Your existing vendors know who is good in adjacent trades. Watch whose trucks are on the well-kept centers in your trade area. Vet before you need: check licensing where the trade requires it, ask for references from comparable properties, and give new vendors a small job before you give them a big one. The goal is not a list in a drawer — it is relationships warm enough that your call gets answered on a Sunday.

## Scope of work: the document that prevents arguments

Most vendor disputes are scope disputes. "Seal the lot" means different things to the owner picturing crack-fill, sealcoat, and restripe, and to the contractor picturing a quick spray of the drive lanes. Write scopes down, every time, for anything beyond a trivial service call.

A workable scope of work is one page: the locations and quantities (square feet, linear feet of crack, number of units), the materials and methods specified, the standard of finish, site protection and cleanup obligations, working hours that respect tenant operations, and the price basis — lump sum or unit pricing. Attach photos and mark them up. For recurring services, the scope lives inside the service agreement; for projects, it lives in the proposal you actually sign, not the one you discussed. When the invoice arrives, the scope is what you check it against.

## Service agreements versus per-call

Some work belongs on contract; some belongs on demand.

Put recurring, predictable work on service agreements: landscaping, parking lot sweeping, pest control, HVAC preventive maintenance, grease and dumpster-area service where applicable. Agreements buy you scheduled attention, steadier pricing, and a vendor who knows the property. Negotiate them annually, specify visit frequency and response times in writing, and keep terms short enough that performance matters to the vendor — one year with renewal options is common practice.

Keep unpredictable repair work per-call: roof leaks, plumbing failures, door and glass repairs. Per-call work is where your bench depth and your scopes do the heavy lifting. The mistake to avoid is drifting into de facto exclusivity — one vendor doing all your per-call HVAC work at whatever rate they name because nobody else has been quoted in three years.

Whichever structure you choose, define emergency response before an emergency tests it. Put after-hours contact numbers, expected response times, and after-hours rates in writing — inside the agreement for contract vendors, inside your vendor file for per-call ones. A Saturday-night roof leak is not the moment to learn what your vendor charges for a weekend callout or whether they answer at all.

## The paperwork: COIs and W-9s

Two documents protect you, and both must exist before work starts.

**Certificates of insurance (COIs).** Every vendor sets foot on the property with general liability insurance at limits you specify — many landlords require $1 million per occurrence as a baseline, though requirements vary — plus workers' compensation where required by law, and, for vendors driving heavy equipment on your lot, auto liability. Ask to be named as additional insured on liability policies for ongoing service vendors. Collect the COI before the first visit, calendar its expiration date, and suspend scheduling when it lapses. A vendor working uninsured on your property converts their accident into your problem.

**W-9s.** Collect a completed W-9 from every vendor before the first payment. You will need it for year-end 1099 reporting, and chasing tax IDs in December is misery. Make it a rule with teeth: no W-9, no check.

## Bid discipline: when to bid, when to keep the incumbent

Competitive bidding keeps pricing honest, but bidding everything is its own failure mode — it burns vendor goodwill, delays urgent work, and optimizes price over the knowledge a good incumbent carries. A workable discipline:

- **Always bid** large projects — repaving, roof replacement, façade work, anything that will move the capital budget. Three bids, same written scope, and compare line by line rather than by total.
- **Periodically bid** recurring service agreements, on a rotation of roughly every two to three years, even if you expect to keep the incumbent. The incumbent should win on value, not on your inertia.
- **Rarely bid** small urgent repairs. Speed and trust are worth more than the last ten percent on a $400 plumbing call.

When bids come in far apart, interrogate the scope before the price. The low bid is often a different job than you specified. And when you keep an incumbent, tell them why — performance history, response time, knowledge of the property — so the standard for keeping the work is explicit.

## Seasonal cycles and performance tracking

Retail vendor work runs on seasons. Build the year around them. In Gulf South markets and similar climates, a typical cycle: HVAC preventive service before the cooling season and again before winter; landscaping on a growing-season schedule with mulching and plantings timed to spring and fall; parking lot sweeping year-round with extra passes after storms; lot sealcoating and striping planned for dry weather windows; roof inspections in spring and after major storms; gutter and drain cleaning before the wet season. Put all of it on the calendar with lead time for bids and scheduling — vendors book out in season.

Then track performance, simply and consistently. For each vendor, keep a file and a scorecard:

**Vendor file checklist:**

- Signed agreement or approved scope for current work
- COI with expiration date calendared
- W-9 on file
- License documentation where the trade requires it
- Pricing history and rate sheets
- Contact and after-hours emergency numbers

**Performance notes, updated after each job:** on-time arrival, quality of finish, cleanup, invoice accuracy against scope, and tenant complaints or compliments. Review the scorecards once a quarter. Patterns emerge fast — the landscaper who is slipping, the HVAC tech whose fixes hold. When you renew, rebid, or replace a vendor, you will be deciding from a record rather than a mood.

### The view from Arnold Blvd

A 70,000 SF legacy center like On The Blvd on Arnold Boulevard in Lafayette leans hard on its bench. An aging roof, a large asphalt lot, Gulf Coast heat and storm seasons, and a fleet of tenant HVAC units mean the same trades return every month of the year. The Operator Landlord approach applies here too: vendors are partners in the property's condition, not just invoice generators. That means written scopes they can succeed against, COIs and W-9s kept current without being chased, seasonal work scheduled before the season arrives, and honest feedback in both directions. The tenants experience the center through the lot they park on and the air conditioning in July — which is to say, through the vendors.

## The practical next step

1. List every trade your center uses and write down your primary and backup vendor for each; fill the gaps this month.
2. Audit your paperwork: pull every active vendor's COI and W-9, and set expiration reminders for every certificate.
3. Put the seasonal calendar on paper — HVAC service, sweeping, landscaping, lot work, roof inspections — with lead times for bidding and scheduling.
4. Pick your two largest recurring agreements and calendar their next competitive bid or renewal review.
5. Start a one-page scorecard per vendor and update it after the next five completed jobs.
