# The Anatomy of a Retail Lease: NNN, Gross, and Modified Gross

A retail lease is not a rent agreement with attachments. It is a bundle of economic, control, and commitment clauses that divide costs, rights, and risks between landlord and tenant for five to ten years at a time. Owners who read leases clause by clause make better deals and avoid the disputes that consume operating time. This article walks the anatomy. It is educational, not legal advice — have your own counsel review any lease you sign.

## Three ways to divide the costs

Lease structure answers one question: who pays the property's operating costs?

- **Triple net (NNN).** The tenant pays base rent plus its pro-rata share of real estate taxes, insurance, and common area maintenance. The landlord's base rent is closer to true net income, and expense inflation passes to tenants. Most multi-tenant shopping centers use NNN.
- **Gross (full service).** The tenant pays one rent figure; the landlord absorbs taxes, insurance, and CAM. Simpler for the tenant, riskier for the landlord in inflationary years, and more common in office than retail.
- **Modified gross.** The middle ground — typically base rent plus some expenses (often taxes and insurance) while the landlord covers others, or a base-year structure where the tenant pays increases over a starting expense level.

Structure choice is a pricing decision. A $20 NNN rent and a $26 gross rent can be the same deal wearing different labels. What matters is which party carries expense volatility — and whether your leases across the center are consistent enough to administer and reconcile cleanly.

## The money clauses

**Term.** Retail terms commonly run three to ten years, often with options. Longer terms stabilize income but lock in today's rent; shorter terms keep repricing power but stack rollover risk.

**Base rent.** Quoted per square foot per year. On a 2,000 SF suite at $18 PSF, base rent is $36,000 annually — an illustrative example, not a market quote.

**Escalations.** Rent grows by fixed annual steps (for example, 3% or $0.50 PSF per year) or by CPI, frequently with floors and caps. Fixed escalations are easier to budget and to reconcile; CPI escalations track inflation but introduce administrative work.

**CAM/NNN pass-throughs.** In NNN leases, tenants reimburse their pro-rata share — usually suite square footage over the center's leasable square footage. The clause details matter: what is in the CAM pool, whether there is an administrative fee, whether caps apply (controllable versus non-controllable expenses), and whether the landlord may gross up variable expenses when occupancy falls. Sloppy pools produce reconciliation fights.

## Percentage rent

Some retail leases add percentage rent: the tenant pays a percent of gross sales above a breakpoint, on top of base rent. A natural breakpoint divides base rent by the percentage rate — at $36,000 base and a 6% rate, the natural breakpoint is $600,000 in annual sales (illustrative). Percentage rent is most common with anchors, grocers, and restaurants, where it gives the landlord upside participation and keeps base rent affordable for the tenant. It comes with obligations: tenant sales reporting, landlord audit rights, and record-keeping definitions of what counts as gross sales. Online fulfillment and returns have made that definition worth negotiating carefully. Set the reporting rhythm in the lease — monthly sales statements within a fixed window, an annual reconciliation, and audit rights with a defined look-back period. Percentage rent only works when both sides trust the numbers, and trust is built by definitions, not goodwill.

## The control clauses

Control clauses allocate rights over what happens in and around the space.

- **Use clause.** Defines what the tenant may sell or do. Narrow uses protect the tenant mix; broad uses give tenants flexibility to evolve. Landlords should keep uses specific enough to enforce mix but not so narrow that a healthy tenant cannot adjust its menu or merchandise.
- **Exclusive use.** Grants a tenant protection against direct competitors in the center. Exclusives are valuable and dangerous: one broad exclusive can block three future tenants. Define the prohibited use tightly, and consider carve-outs for existing tenants and anchor lease rights.
- **Co-tenancy.** Lets a tenant reduce rent or terminate if an anchor closes or occupancy falls below a threshold. These clauses transfer anchor risk to the landlord; know exactly which tenants have them and what triggers them.
- **Assignment and subletting.** Controls whether the tenant can transfer the lease. Landlords typically require consent, often "not to be unreasonably withheld," sometimes with recapture rights — the landlord's option to take the space back instead. This clause decides whether a failing tenant can hand you a replacement or leave you with a dark suite.

Control clauses interact. An exclusive granted to a strong tenant today can collide with the assignment of another lease tomorrow, and a co-tenancy remedy can convert an anchor vacancy into a center-wide rent reset. Read them together, not in isolation.

## The commitment clauses

- **Renewal options.** A tenant option extends the term at preset or formula rent, with strict notice windows — often six to twelve months before expiration. Missed notice deadlines are a common, expensive dispute.
- **TI and landlord work.** Tenant improvement allowances are landlord dollars toward buildout, quoted per square foot, or the landlord delivers a defined scope ("turnkey" or "vanilla shell"). The clause should state who owns the improvements, disbursement conditions, and lien protections.
- **Guarantees.** Personal or corporate guarantees back the lease. They often burn off — reduce or expire — after a period of clean payment history. A guarantee from an entity with no assets is decoration; underwrite the guarantor, not the signature.
- **Default and remedies.** Defines events of default, cure periods, and landlord remedies. Short cure periods and clear late-fee and interest terms keep small problems from becoming long ones.

### The view from Arnold Blvd

A 70,000 SF legacy center like On The Blvd carries leases written in different decades, often on different forms. The Operator Landlord's job is standardization at renewal: consistent CAM pool definitions, current escalation mechanics, exclusive clauses that match today's mix rather than 1998's. An abstracted lease summary for every suite — term, options, exclusives, co-tenancy triggers — is the control panel for the whole property.

## Reading leases you inherited

Buying a center means buying its leases, and paper written by someone else's lawyer decades ago rarely matches how the center is actually being run. Closing the gap between the two is real work.

Start with abstraction: read every lease and every amendment, and reduce each to a one-page summary — parties, dates, rent schedule, escalations, options, expense obligations, exclusives, co-tenancy, guarantees. Then compare the abstract to the billing. Common findings: tenants paying old rent because an escalation was never applied, CAM pools billed differently than the lease defines, options exercised informally by letter, and side agreements living in email threads. Each gap is either money left uncollected or a liability the seller left behind.

Two cautions. First, the lease wins: what the signed document says governs, not what the prior owner practiced, so do not assume current billing is correct. Second, fix gaps prospectively and professionally — a tenant who has underbilled CAM for five years does not deserve an ambush; they deserve a clean explanation, corrected billing, and reasonable time to adjust. An estoppel certificate, in which the tenant confirms the lease's terms and status, is the standard tool for locking down what the file actually says at acquisition and again at refinancing.

## The practical next step

1. Abstract every lease in your center onto a one-page summary: dates, rent schedule, options, exclusives, co-tenancy, guarantees.
2. Map every exclusive use and co-tenancy clause on a single sheet — these constrain your next three leases.
3. Compare CAM pool language across leases and flag inconsistencies before the next reconciliation.
4. Calendar every option notice deadline with reminders 90 days early.
5. Have counsel refresh your standard lease form against how you actually operate today.
