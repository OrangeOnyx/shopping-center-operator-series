# The Sale Process: Brokers, Offering Memorandums, and Closing

Selling a shopping center is a process with a known shape: choose representation, set pricing guidance, package the property, run a marketing period, negotiate from letter of intent to purchase agreement, survive due diligence, and close. Owners who know the shape keep control of it. This article walks each stage from the seller's side, including what to demand from a broker, what belongs in the offering memorandum, and the document room that makes diligence boring. It is educational process knowledge, not legal or investment advice — your attorney and advisors run your actual transaction.

## Selecting a broker

The broker is the highest-leverage hire in the sale, and the selection deserves the same rigor as hiring any key vendor.

Interview at least two or three candidates, and weight these factors:

- **Relevant product experience.** Retail, and ideally centers of your size and type, in your region. The buyer universe for a neighborhood center is specific; a broker who knows it by name is worth more than a famous brand that does not.
- **A credible pricing view.** Each candidate should present a broker opinion of value (BOV) — their estimate of probable selling price with supporting logic. Beware the highest number. A BOV inflated to win the listing leads to a stale listing and a price cut that reads as distress.
- **A marketing plan in writing.** Who they call first, how they package, what the timeline looks like, how they handle confidentiality with tenants and competitors.
- **Alignment on communication.** Weekly reporting during marketing, immediate reporting of offers, and honest advice when the market disagrees with your expectations.

The listing agreement itself deserves legal review: term length, commission structure and what triggers it, exclusivity scope, and cancellation rights are all negotiable and all matter.

## Pricing guidance and the offering memorandum

Pricing a shopping center is an informed judgment, not a formula. Buyers typically anchor on the property's net operating income (NOI) — income after operating expenses, before debt service and capital items — divided by a capitalization rate, or cap rate, that reflects market conditions, asset quality, and risk. What cap rate applies to your center depends on factors that vary by market and by moment: tenancy durability, lease term remaining, physical condition, and buyer appetite. Treat any single number as a hypothesis to be tested against the broker's read and your own homework.

The offering memorandum (OM) is the property's case in writing. A strong OM for a neighborhood or community center includes:

- **Executive summary and investment highlights** — what the center is and why the income is durable.
- **The rent roll and financials** — trailing income and expense, CAM reconciliation history, and any identified one-time items.
- **Tenancy detail** — lease terms, expirations, options, and tenant descriptions accurate enough to survive diligence.
- **Physical and location story** — site plan, photos that are current and honest, traffic and corridor context, capital history.
- **The process** — how offers are to be submitted, what documentation is available, and the expected timeline.

Read the OM line by line before it goes out. Every error in it becomes a credibility tax in diligence.

## Marketing period and LOI to PSA

A typical marketing period runs several weeks to a few months, depending on the market and the asset. Expect a rhythm: the OM goes to a curated buyer list, calls and site visits follow, and interested parties submit either letters of intent (LOIs) or formal offers by a call-for-offers date if the process is structured that way.

The LOI is non-binding on price and terms but binding on your attention. Evaluate beyond price: the buyer's track record of closing, their capital source and whether the offer depends on financing, the diligence period length, the earnest money and when it goes non-refundable, and the proposed closing timeline. A lower price from a buyer who closes is often worth more than a higher price from one who retrades — the industry term for renegotiating price mid-diligence.

From accepted LOI, attorneys draft the purchase and sale agreement (PSA), the binding contract. Seller-side points that matter: diligence length and what happens when it expires, the size and staging of earnest money, representations you are asked to make and their survival period, and conditions to closing. Your attorney owns this document; your job is to understand every obligation you are signing.

## Buyer due diligence, from the seller's side

Diligence is where prepared sellers win. The buyer's team will examine leases, financials, physical condition, title, and environmental status, and every gap becomes a request, a delay, or a price conversation.

Two instruments deserve special attention because the seller drives them:

- **Estoppel certificates.** Signed statements from each tenant confirming lease terms, rent, deposits, and the absence of defaults or disputes. Buyers and their lenders require them from most or all tenants. Start early, keep requests accurate and narrow, and follow up persistently — tenants return estoppels on their own schedule, and a center with cooperative tenants closes faster.
- **SNDAs.** Subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment agreements, through which a tenant's lease interacts with the buyer's or lender's position — the tenant agrees to recognize a new owner or lender, and in return typically receives assurance their tenancy survives a foreclosure. Major tenants and lenders often require them.

Physical diligence — roof, structure, HVAC, lot, environmental — goes better when the prep work from the twelve-month plan has already removed the surprises. Your posture throughout: respond fast, document everything, and fix what is cheap to fix rather than negotiating around it.

## Financing contingencies and closing logistics

Most buyers of small and mid-size centers use debt, so their financing is your timeline risk. Watch the debt milestones as closely as your own obligations: appraisal, lender diligence, commitment, and the rate environment that can move a buyer's math between LOI and close. Terms vary by market and lender, and this article will not forecast rates — the seller's defense is not prediction but structure: meaningful earnest money that goes non-refundable at diligence expiry, defined financing deadlines, and a backup conversation kept warm when possible.

Closing itself is choreography: the deed and assignment documents, prorations of rent, CAM, taxes, and insurance through the closing date, transfer of deposits and service contracts, keys and codes, tenant notification letters, and the final settlement statement. Your attorney and the title or escrow company run the mechanics. Your checklist is simpler: know every item leaving with the property, every dollar crossing, and every obligation that survives closing — and read the settlement statement twice.

## The seller's document-room checklist

Assemble this before marketing, update it through diligence:

- **Leases:** every lease, amendment, guaranty, commencement letter, and side letter, organized by tenant.
- **Financials:** trailing twelve months plus two prior years of P&L, CAM reconciliations, current rent roll, and aging report.
- **Tenant files:** insurance certificates, estoppels as returned, SNDA status, correspondence affecting terms.
- **Physical:** capital history schedule, roof and HVAC records, warranties, service contracts, inspection reports.
- **Title and legal:** survey, title policy, exceptions documents, entity documents and authority to sell, zoning and permit records.
- **Environmental:** Phase I reports and any follow-up, plus records of resolved issues.
- **Operational:** utility accounts, vendor list, signage and access details, keys and codes inventory.

A complete document room does two jobs: it shortens diligence, and it signals an owner whose numbers can be trusted — which is worth real money at the negotiation table.

### The view from Arnold Blvd

For a roughly 70,000 SF legacy multi-tenant center like On The Blvd at 101–149 Arnold Blvd in Lafayette, Louisiana — owned by Belle Realty of Lafayette, LLC and run under the Operator Landlord approach — the process above is proportionate, not aspirational. A center this size sells to buyers who read documents carefully and walk the property in person, and an owner who already runs clean books, current signage, and cooperative tenant relationships arrives at LOI with most of the diligence answers already in the file. The Operator Landlord's daily habits are, in effect, a standing document room.

## The practical next step

1. Interview two or three retail-experienced brokers and compare their BOVs and marketing plans — not just their price opinions.
2. Build the document room from the checklist above before any marketing begins.
3. Draft estoppel request templates with your attorney so tenant outreach starts on day one of diligence.
4. Decide your evaluation criteria for offers in advance: price, certainty, timeline, and retrade risk.
5. Put your attorney and CPA on notice early — the best time to engage them is before the LOI, not after.
