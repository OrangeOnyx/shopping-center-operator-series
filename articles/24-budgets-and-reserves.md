# Budgets and Reserves: Planning for Roofs, Parking Lots, and Surprises

A shopping center budget is not an accounting exercise. It is a set of decisions made in advance: what the property will earn, what it will cost to run, what must be set aside for the big-ticket items, and what happens to the plan when something goes wrong. Centers that budget well don't avoid surprises — roofs still fail and tenants still leave — but they absorb them without crisis. This article walks through the annual budgeting process, reserve logic, and stress-testing, using illustrative numbers only.

## The annual budgeting process

A workable budget cycle for a multi-tenant retail center starts 60 to 90 days before the fiscal year and builds from evidence, not wishful thinking:

1. **Start with history.** Pull the last 24 months of actuals by line item. Two years matters because one year can hide seasonality — landscaping costs in a Gulf South growing season, for instance, look nothing like winter months.
2. **Get vendor bids early.** Landscaping, sweeping, pressure washing, security, pest control: request renewal pricing or competitive bids in the fall, not in January. Contracted services are the most knowable part of the budget if you ask before you finalize.
3. **Set escalation assumptions explicitly.** Insurance premiums, utilities, and labor rates move. Rather than applying a blanket percentage, assign an escalation assumption per line — and write the assumption down. When actuals deviate, you'll know which assumption failed instead of wondering what happened. Terms and rates vary by market and year; use your own quotes, not generic indices.
4. **Build the leasing assumptions.** This is where budgets live or die. For each vacant or expiring suite, state: expected lease-up or renewal date, assumed rent, free-rent concession, tenant improvement (TI) allowance, and leasing commission (LC). Then haircut it. Renewal assumptions in the 70–80% probability range for stable centers are a common planning posture, but your retention record should drive your number.
5. **Lock the reimbursement math.** In NNN structures, CAM/tax/insurance estimates billed to tenants must reconcile to the budget. The budget and the CAM estimate letter are the same document viewed from two sides.

Deliver the budget, and any lease-required tenant estimates, before the year starts. Late budgets are not just sloppy; in many leases they waive or complicate reimbursement rights depending on the lease language.

## Reserve study logic

Operating budgets handle the year. Reserves handle the decade. The tool that connects them is a reserve study — a structured inventory of every major component, its remaining useful life, and its replacement cost.

The logic is straightforward:

- **Inventory the components.** Roof, parking lot (overlay and full rebuild as separate events), HVAC units, pylon and monument signs, exterior paint and façade, lighting, plumbing and electrical mains.
- **Estimate remaining life.** Use vendor inspections, not guesses. A roofer's written assessment of remaining service life is worth more than any spreadsheet formula.
- **Price replacement in today's dollars,** then escalate to the expected replacement year at a stated inflation assumption.
- **Divide the future cost by the months remaining.** That is the required monthly funding for that component. Sum across components for the total reserve requirement.

A simplified illustrative example (not any actual property): a roof section with 8 years of life left and an estimated $160,000 replacement cost in future dollars requires roughly $1,650 per month of funding. A parking lot overlay in 5 years at $120,000 adds $2,000 per month. A fleet of rooftop HVAC units staggered over the next decade might add $1,500–$2,500. The total — here around $5,200 per month, or roughly $62,000 a year — is the property's true cost of staying whole. An illustrative center producing $570,000 of NOI should expect reserves of this order to claim a meaningful share of it.

The point is not the arithmetic, which any spreadsheet can do. The point is that the number exists before the failure does.

## Operating reserves vs. capital reserves

Keep two separate buckets, and be honest about which is which:

- **Capital reserves** fund the reserve-study schedule: predictable, dated, component-by-component replacements. This money is spoken for; it just hasn't left yet.
- **Operating reserves** fund the unpredictable: a major vacancy that takes nine months to backfill, a deductible after a storm, a tenant default mid-lease. A common planning target is several months of operating expenses plus debt service; the right level depends on your rollover schedule, tenant concentration, and lender requirements, which vary by loan.

The failure mode to avoid is raiding one bucket for the other. A capital reserve quietly used to cover operating shortfalls is a roof failure deferred to a worse moment. If operating shortfalls are chronic, the problem is the operations or the rent roll — not the reserve policy — and it deserves its own decision.

## Funding monthly as a discipline

Reserve funding fails when it is residual — "whatever's left at year-end." Nothing is ever left. The discipline that works is treating reserve funding as a fixed monthly transfer, like debt service:

- Set an automatic monthly transfer to a separate reserve account on the same day rent collections settle.
- Report reserves on the monthly financial package: beginning balance, funding in, draws out, ending balance, and funded percentage against the reserve study.
- When a draw happens — the repave, the roof section — record it against the component it funded. This keeps the study honest and makes the next update easy.

If your loan includes lender-controlled reserve escrows, reconcile them against your own study. Lender escrows are often sized to the lender's minimum comfort, not to your property's actual component schedule. The gap is yours to fund.

## Stress-testing the budget

A budget that only works if everything goes right isn't a budget. Before finalizing, run at least three downside cases:

**Case 1 — A major vacancy.** Take your largest or likeliest rollover and assume the suite sits empty for nine months. Remove the base rent *and* the reimbursements (empty suites stop paying their CAM/tax/insurance share), add back the unrecovered expense burden, and add TI/LC for the eventual backfill. On an illustrative center with $570,000 NOI, losing a $90,000-a-year tenant for nine months plus $60,000 of re-tenanting cost can swing cash flow by well over $100,000. Does the operating reserve absorb it? For how long?

**Case 2 — An HVAC fleet failure.** Assume several rooftop units fail in the same season — plausible for same-vintage equipment on a legacy center. At an illustrative $6,000–$12,000 per replacement, four units is $24,000–$48,000 in one quarter. If the capital reserve covers it, the year is fine. If it doesn't, the budget was optimistic.

**Case 3 — Insurance premium shock.** Premiums in many markets, particularly coastal and Gulf South markets, have moved sharply in recent years. Model a 20–30% premium increase at renewal and a higher wind/hail deductible. The premium hit is a permanent NOI reduction; the deductible change is a new operating-reserve requirement.

For each case, write down the response in advance: which spending gets deferred, which reserve gets drawn, and at what point ownership injects capital. Deciding the sequence during the stress test is much cheaper than deciding it during the emergency.

## The practical next step

1. **Pull 24 months of actuals** and lay them next to this year's budget; circle every line that missed by more than 10% and write down why.
2. **Commission a component inventory.** Get written remaining-life assessments for the roof, lot, and HVAC fleet from your vendors.
3. **Build or update the reserve study** and compute the required monthly funding. Compare it to what you actually set aside last year.
4. **Open a separate reserve account** and automate the monthly transfer if you haven't.
5. **Run one stress test** this month — the major-vacancy case is usually the most revealing — and document the response plan.
