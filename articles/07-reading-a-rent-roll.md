# Reading a Rent Roll: What the Numbers Actually Tell You

The rent roll is the single most-read document in a retail acquisition, and the most under-read. Buyers scan the total and move on. Operators read every column, because each one answers a different question about the quality and durability of the income. This article decodes the columns, then walks through four calculations — weighted average lease term, occupancy, rollover exposure, and rent concentration — using a small illustrative example.

## What a rent roll is — and is not

A rent roll is a snapshot: who occupies the center, on what terms, as of a date. It is a summary prepared by the owner or manager, which means it is only as accurate as the lease file behind it. In diligence, the rent roll is the map; the leases are the territory. Every material line on the roll should tie to a signed document, and every discrepancy is a question for the seller.

A rent roll is also not a collections report. It typically shows contract rent — what leases say tenants owe — not what was actually paid. A center can show full occupancy on the roll and still have a collections problem. Pair the roll with a delinquency or aging report before drawing conclusions about income.

## Decoding the columns

A standard retail rent roll carries these fields, and each one earns its place:

- **Tenant and suite.** Identification, plus suite location. Suite position matters: endcaps, inlines, and pads lease at different rates and to different tenant types.
- **Square footage (SF).** The denominator for almost every ratio in retail. Confirm whether figures are rentable or usable, and whether the center's total matches the survey.
- **Base rent and rent per SF.** Base rent divided by SF gives the rate that lets you compare tenants, suites, and competing centers. Rent per SF is the language of retail leasing.
- **NNN charges.** The tenant's share of taxes, insurance, and common area maintenance, usually quoted per SF. A tenant at $14.00 base plus $4.50 NNN occupies very differently from one at $18.50 gross.
- **Lease start and expiration.** The raw material for term and rollover analysis.
- **Options.** Renewal options, with their notice windows and pricing. An option at a fixed rate is a tenant asset and a landlord ceiling; note them.
- **Escalations.** Annual or periodic increases — flat steps, CPI-linked, or percentage bumps. Escalations determine whether income keeps pace with expenses over the hold.
- **Percentage rent.** Some retail tenants pay additional rent as a percentage of sales above a breakpoint. Percentage rent signals tenant health: a tenant paying overage is, by definition, selling well.

## An illustrative example

The table below is **illustrative only** — a hypothetical six-tenant strip within a 70,000 SF neighborhood center, drawn to demonstrate the calculations.

| Tenant | Suite | SF | Base rent/SF | Annual base | Start | Expiry | Options | Escalation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Anchor grocer | A | 30,000 | $9.00 | $270,000 | 2019 | 2034 | 2×5 yr | 10% / 5 yr |
| National pharmacy | B | 12,000 | $16.00 | $192,000 | 2021 | 2031 | 2×5 yr | CPI |
| Restaurant (pad) | C | 4,000 | $24.00 | $96,000 | 2023 | 2028 | 1×5 yr | 3% / yr |
| Hair salon | D | 1,500 | $20.00 | $30,000 | 2024 | 2027 | None | 3% / yr |
| Dry cleaner | E | 1,800 | $18.00 | $32,400 | 2020 | 2026 | 1×3 yr | Flat |
| Vacant | F | 3,200 | — | — | — | — | — | — |

Total SF: 52,500. Occupied: 49,300. Annual base rent in place: $620,400.

## The four calculations

**Weighted average lease term (WALT).** WALT measures how long the income is contractually committed. Weight each tenant's remaining term by its base rent and divide by total rent. From the illustrative table, remaining terms (assume mid-2026) are roughly 8, 5, 2, 1, and 0.5 years:

(270,000 × 8 + 192,000 × 5 + 96,000 × 2 + 30,000 × 1 + 32,400 × 0.5) ÷ 620,400 ≈ **5.4 years**

Lenders and buyers typically prefer a WALT above five years on anchored retail. A short WALT means you are buying leasing work, not just income.

**Occupancy: by SF versus by unit.** Occupancy by SF is occupied square footage divided by total: 49,300 ÷ 52,500 ≈ **93.9%**. Occupancy by unit is occupied suites divided by total suites: 5 ÷ 6 ≈ **83.3%**. Both are true and they tell different stories. A center can be 95% occupied by SF while several small shops sit dark — the unit number reveals the leasing workload, the SF number reveals the income coverage.

**Rollover exposure by year.** Total the rent (or SF) expiring in each future year and divide by the whole. In the example, 2026–2028 expirations — dry cleaner, salon, and restaurant — total $158,400, or roughly **26% of base rent inside three years**. That is the leasing exposure you inherit. Heavy near-term rollover is negotiating leverage on price and a to-do list on day one of ownership.

**Rent concentration.** The top tenant's share of total base rent: 270,000 ÷ 620,400 ≈ **43.5%** in this example. Concentration cuts both ways. A credit anchor at 40%+ of rent can support better financing — until its lease approaches expiry, at which point the same concentration becomes the deal's central risk. Track the top tenant and the top three as standing metrics.

## What the rent roll does not tell you

Four realities hide behind a clean-looking roll. First, **month-to-month and holdover tenants**. A suite shown as occupied may be a tenant past expiry on thirty days' notice — occupancy that can vanish before your first mortgage payment. Check every expiry date against today.

Second, **reimbursement behavior**. The roll shows NNN charges billed; it does not show whether caps, gross-up clauses, or excluded-cost provisions in individual leases prevent full recovery. Two tenants at identical $4.50 NNN rates can produce very different collections.

Third, **sales and health data**. Rent rolls rarely include tenant sales, yet sales relative to rent — the occupancy cost ratio — is the best predictor of who renews and who defaults. Ask for sales reports where leases require them; ask tenants directly where they do not.

Fourth, **artificially supported income**. A seller who signed a tenant at an above-market rate with a large concession six months before listing has engineered the roll. Free rent, moving allowances, and unamortized tenant improvements live in the lease file, not the rent roll — one more reason the roll is the map and the leases are the territory.

### The view from Arnold Blvd

On The Blvd Shopping Center is roughly 70,000 square feet of multi-tenant retail in Lafayette, owned and operated under an Operator Landlord model. For a center of that size, these four numbers are not an acquisition exercise — they are a monthly dashboard. WALT tells the operator how much lease term is in the bank. Rollover by year sets the leasing calendar. Concentration says how much of the income walks out the door if one tenant stumbles. A rent roll reviewed this way stops being a report and becomes an early-warning system.

## The practical next step

1. Rebuild your current rent roll in a spreadsheet with the columns above, tied line-by-line to the lease file.
2. Compute WALT, occupancy by SF and by unit, rollover by year for the next five years, and top-tenant concentration.
3. Add delinquency and collections columns so contract rent and collected rent sit side by side.
4. Flag every lease expiring within 24 months and assign each a plan: renew, replace, or reposition.
5. Refresh the calculations monthly and carry them into every lender and partner conversation — the operator who knows these numbers cold negotiates from strength.
